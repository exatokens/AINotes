The Lossy Nature of Parsing and Chunking
In the architecture of enterprise Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems, stan-
dard operations such as parsing, chunking, and embedding are heavily relied upon. How-
ever, it is critical to recognize that each of these steps is inherently a lossy operation.
Every time a continuous piece of knowledge is fractured into chunks, contextual fidelity
is destroyed.
1.1
The Vision Language Model Paradigm
The orthodoxy that text must always be parsed, chunked, and embedded must be funda-
mentally questioned. When comparing methodologies using dense technical texts, such
as Christopher Bishop’s Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning (PRML) textbook,
an approach utilizing a Vision Language Model at the page level, completely avoiding
text parsing and granular chunking, often yields vastly superior retrieval results. This
empirical evidence forces the architect to constantly ask: What is lost by parsing this
data?
1.2
The Centroid Delusion in Large Chunks
When chunking text, selecting the appropriate boundary size presents a mathematical
paradox. Utilizing large chunks, such as the default 512 tokens, representing roughly a
full page of text, triggers a severe failure mode known as the “centroid delusion.”
Text is linear, containing multiple distinct semantic units of thought arranged se-
quentially. Consider a sequence of individual thoughts within a single chunk, denoted as

1.3
Contextual Hemorrhaging in Small Chunks
Attempting to resolve the centroid delusion by enforcing very small, granular chunks
introduces a different, equally destructive problem: the total loss of implicit and explicit
context. This manifests primarily through two linguistic breakdowns:

1. Anaphora and Cataphora: Technical texts heavily utilize pronouns and ref-
erences (e.g., “it,” “they,” “this”) that refer to entities outside arbitrary chunk
boundaries. A chunk reading, “Considering equation 3.6 and the figure above, we
therefore conclude...” is functionally meaningless when retrieved in isolation.
2. Implicit Contextual Consequence: Narrative environments are severed. For
example, if a text reads, “I entered the room. There was a beautiful chandelier,” a
small chunk containing only the second sentence entirely loses the locational context
of where the chandelier exists.
1.4
Foundational Contextual Repairs
Because neither large nor small chunks perfectly preserve knowledge, specific repair mech-
anisms must be introduced:
• Hierarchical Chunking: Leveraging markdown structures explicitly provided by
the author (chapters, sections, subsections) to establish coarse-grained boundaries
before applying smaller chunking. This respects the author’s intentional semantic
shifts.
• Semantic Chunking: Avoiding arbitrary token limits to instead best-effort isolate
atomic units of thought.
• Contextual Chunking and Late Interaction: Re-injecting lost context. This
includes utilizing an LLM to prepend surrounding context to isolated chunks, or
employing late interaction architectures where chunking occurs only after trans-
former self-attention mechanisms have allowed every token to absorb the context
of the entire text sequence.

The Fundamental Disconnect in Search Optimization
Engineers frequently fall into the trap of doing efficiently what should not be done at all.
The core failure of many RAG systems stems from a profound misunderstanding of what
the system must actually optimize for.

2.1
Optimizing for the Exam: The Analogy of Meera and Ravi
Consider two students preparing for highly competitive physics entrance examinations,
such as the IIT-JEE in India or the Gaokao in China.
The first student, Meera, possesses a deep love for the subject. When encountering
thermodynamics, she meticulously studies the Maxwell-Boltzmann equation, optimizing
her entire workflow for a profound, theoretical understanding of the material.
The second student, Ravi, has no inherent love for the subject. Instead of reading the
textbook, he focuses exclusively on the exam format. He gathers a definitive list of 20
to 25 frequently asked questions and memorizes the exact procedural steps required to
answer them perfectly. He creates a direct mapping from anticipated query to optimal
response.
Statistically, Ravi is significantly more likely to clear the exam, complete it well under
the time limit, and achieve the highest scores. While Meera optimized for understanding,
Ravi optimized for the test itself.
Search is the exam. A user approaching a RAG system provides a query seeking
a rapid, specific answer.
A system that optimizes for procedural retrieval, mapping
anticipated queries to precise answers, will vastly outperform a system that simply indexes
raw pedagogical text.
2.2
Document Registers vs. Search Intent
The fundamental disconnect in enterprise search lies in the purpose and audience for
which documents are originally authored. Documents are written in specific structural
“registers” that actively resist search engines:
• Textbooks: Target learners who seek knowledge sequentially. They are didactic
masterpieces that introduce concepts carefully to avoid cyclic references. They are
not written in a question-and-answer format as the competitive exams coaching
center study materials do.
• Research Papers: Target specialized cabals of peer researchers.
They follow
a rigid IMRAD structure (Introduction, Method, Results, Discussion). If a user
asks how a specific development leads to better interpretability, the answer is not
localized. The introduction states the problem (e.g., LLM reasoning traces hiding
true thoughts), the method details autoencoders converting logits to English via
reconstruction loss, and the results section contains the performance metrics (e.g.,
a 9% improvement). The answer is scattered across the entire document.
• Legal Contracts: Target adversarial lawyers.
Written in watertight legalese,
these documents rely heavily on interdependent clauses (“Notwithstanding clause

2.3
The Missing Audience
None of these document registers are written for a search engine. A search engine acts
as a proxy for a user with a specific, knowledge-seeking query. Simply chunking and
tokenizing raw text assumes the answer is waiting in a single, perfectly phrased block.
In reality, the synthesis of thoughts required to answer the query is rarely present in the
raw chunk. To make a corpus truly alive and eloquent, it must be completely repurposed
to speak the language of the seeker.

Derivative Artifacts for Knowledge Transformation
Because raw text is hostile to search, the corpus must be fundamentally transformed.
Derivative Artifacts are secondary, specialized indices generated from the raw corpus,
optimized explicitly for retrieval performance.
3.1
Proposition-Level Factoids and Point Queries
When users execute point queries for single facts, the centroid delusion causes dense
paragraphs to fail. The solution is to shred the text down into atomic “factoids”, the
smallest, indivisible units of meaning that cannot be further decomposed.
Consider the following dense paragraph:
“Berlin, situated on the banks of the River Spree, serves as the capital of Germany and
is its most populous city, with a metropolitan population exceeding six million residents.
The city has been a center of European politics since reunification in 1990.”
If a user asks, “What is the capital of Germany?” (embedded as vector ⃗eq), comparing
this query against the entire dense paragraph (⃗epara) yields a poor semantic alignment:

3.2
Passage Rewrites and Register Alignment
When a corpus is written in a complicated or adversarial register, it must be rewritten
into a user-friendly format. When non-experts consult legal documents, they do not want
answers delivered in legalese.

For example, a raw passage states:
“Notwithstanding the provisions of section 12B, the licenser retains the irrevocable
right to terminate this agreement upon 30 calendar days written notice.”
A search-friendly passage rewrite translates this to:
“The licenser can terminate the agreement with 30 days written notice regardless of

This rewrite increases the utility value of the knowledge, aligns it with the natural

3.3
Anticipatory Question and Answer Pairs
Because humans interface with RAG systems via questions, the ultimate optimization is
to transmute the entire corpus into queries in advance. Using an LLM, 3 to 8 questions
can be harvested per chunk of text.
For a technical product manual, anticipating queries involves extracting explicit pa-
rameters: “How many ports does it have?” (48 ports plus two uplinks), “What is the
bandwidth?”, or “When was this released?” Generating these pairs anticipates the user’s
need and ensures perfect alignment during retrieval.
3.4
The Meta-Strategy of Contextual Synthesis
The most valuable derivative artifacts are questions extracted from a passage where the
answer resides elsewhere, or requires the synthesis of the entire document.
Consider the statistical mechanics representation of the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribu-
tion found in machine learning probabilities:
p(E) = e−E/kT
Z
(4)
Where Z represents the partition function, and log Z serves as a generator of statistical
moments (mean, standard deviation, skew, kurtosis).
A textbook may present the equation in one section, define the partition function
chapters later, and discuss moments elsewhere. If a user asks a meta-question: “Explain
why this equation is ubiquitous in machine learning,” the answer is highly scattered.
At inference time, sending 20,000 tokens (an entire chapter) to an LLM to synthesize
this answer is far too computationally expensive and slow.
However, by anticipating
this question during the indexing phase, the system can iteratively query its own corpus,
synthesize a comprehensive answer, and save it. This upfront computational cost is amor-
tized across the lifetime of the application, guaranteeing lightning-fast, highly complex
synthesis in production.

Implementation and System Architecture
Vector Database Schema for Artifacts
Implementing these artifacts requires a
precise metadata architecture within the vector database (e.g., Qdrant1). When an An-
ticipatory QA pair is generated, it is stored as a tuple: ⟨q, a⟩.
The Question (q) is passed through the embedder to create the query vector (⃗eq). The
corresponding Answer (ans) and its citation lineage are not embedded, but stored within
the vector’s payload (metadata) as a hashmap:
• question = q
• answer = ans
• source chunks = list[...]
• document = d
When a user’s prompt semantically matches the embedded question, the system re-
trieves the pre-synthesized answer directly from the payload. This architecture bypasses
live generation times and provides perfect, auditable citations back to the exact source
chunks.

Research papers inherently utilize abstracts to provide a structural outline (IMRAD) of

The Path to Production Excellence
4.1
Managing Corpus Updates and Compute Footprints
Enterprise knowledge is not static. Vector databases natively support concurrent reads
and writes, allowing continuous ingestion. The metadata schema heavily simplifies index
management. If “Version 1” of a document is updated, the system executes a delete
command filtering for the specific document = d ID. This instantly purges all associated
raw chunks, QA pairs, factoids, and rewrites, clearing the path for the new version.
Despite the proliferation of derivative indices, the compute footprint remains highly
manageable. A typical enterprise index might contain 100,000 documents resulting in 100
million chunks. For a vector database, managing 100 million items is computationally
trivial.
The indices require vast storage and memory, both of which are inexpensive
commodities, and can readily run on standard CPU architectures. GPU resources are
effectively reserved solely for the upfront generation of the embeddings.
4.2
Overcoming the “RAG-in-a-Box” Betrayal
There is a massive disparity between an average, hastily constructed RAG system and
a world-class architecture. Marketing narratives from cloud providers frequently sell the
illusion that RAG is a solved, simplistic mechanism: “Give us your documents, you’re
done.”
True RAG development is brutally rigorous. It sits at the very beginning of its growth
S-curve, with researchers continuously publishing solutions to unsolved, highly complex
retrieval problems.
Building a generic system yields unpredictable, inconsistent user
experiences. A high-value RAG system is a cash cow for an enterprise, and achieving
frontier-level quality requires painstaking, domain-specific engineering, custom embed-
ding models trained specifically on the target corpus, and the exhaustive generation of
derivative artifacts.

5
The Nature of Derived Artifacts
5.1
The Relationship Between Derived Artifacts and Raw Sources
In the architecture of a sophisticated search or agentic Retrieval-Augmented Generation
(RAG) system, relying solely on raw text chunks severely limits the capacity to serve
complex queries. While the foundational evidence naturally resides in the raw corpus,
derived artifacts, such as summaries, factoids, or synthesized answers, are necessary to
process and surface that evidence effectively.
However, using derived artifacts introduces a critical risk: they can inadvertently
obscure the original evidence, making it difficult to trace the provenance of the data.
Therefore, an absolute architectural rule must be enforced: Every derived artifact
utilized within a search engine must maintain a metadata reference pointing
directly back to the original source, the exact raw chunk, chapter, or docu-
ment. Quoting the specific citation is paramount. This bidirectional pointer from the
derived artifact back to the origin builds critical confidence and ensures the groundedness
of the RAG system.
5.2
Structure of the Retrieved Response
When a response is generated by the Large Language Model (LLM) utilizing this ar-
chitecture, it must not simply return a block of textual answers. A highly structured
response inherently contains three distinct elements:
1. The Answer.
2. The Citations (pointing to the original raw sources).
3. Key Highlights (the best extracts or abstracts derived from the artifacts).
Consider a query concerning the Maxwell-Boltzmann equation. The raw textual ex-
planations may be dense, highly mathematical, and scattered across multiple documents.
A derived artifact provides immediate “Key Highlights” that contextualize the subject:
• It is commonly found in machine learning.
• It utilizes a partition function, where the log partition acts as the generator.
• The Softmax function is a primary example of it.
• It belongs to the exponential family and can be recast as an exponential distribution.
These precise highlights connect the mathematical concept to a broader body of knowl-
edge, an integration that would rarely exist in a single raw document written in those

exact terms. The profound advantage of derived artifacts is the ability to directly an-
swer a user’s question, strictly cite the grounded source via a metadata pointer, and
simultaneously present key extracts that immediately get to the core point.
The insistence on this tripartite structure, Answer, Citations, and Key Highlights, is
not a novel addition but a foundational Day One principle of robust retrieval architecture.
This exact structural discipline was demonstrated in foundational architectural examples,
historically referred to within development frameworks as the “RAG on the Animals”
system and the “RAG to Riches” retrieval model. Even in those early architectures, the
LLM was strictly constrained to never return an isolated answer without its corresponding
grounded citations and derived highlights.
5.3
Computational Economics: The “Pre-Cooked” Paradigm
Generating derivative artifacts fundamentally simplifies the workload of the generative
subsystem. This dynamic mirrors the difference between preparing a meal from first prin-
ciples when severely time-crunched versus utilizing frozen, pre-cooked microwave dinners.
By supplying the RAG generator with derived artifacts, the system is fed semantic ele-
ments that are effectively “pre-cooked.”
The computational and cost implications are enormous. Creating derivative artifacts
requires upfront processing during the ingestion phase. However, failing to do so forces
the agentic RAG system to perform this heavy computational lifting at inference time,
exactly when the user submits a query. If the system must iteratively pull disjointed
chunks, reason over them, and synthesize a coherent solution on the fly, it causes severe
latency and burns expensive compute cycles on high-end hardware. Furthermore, as the
next query arrives, which will inevitably share factual overlap with previous queries, this
expensive process is wastefully repeated. Given the foundational premise that there is
nothing new under the sun and incoming queries inherently overlap in factual answers,
it is vastly more efficient to pre-compute these derivative artifacts and store them for
immediate retrieval.

Knowledge Granularity and Scale-Dependent Emergence
6.1
Levels of Semantic Granularity
Derivative artifacts exist across multiple levels of granularity, each designed to serve a
distinct semantic function during retrieval:
• Factoids: The most granular and atomic level. A factoid is a self-contained se-
mantic unit.
It possesses distinct characteristics, making it highly effective at
resolving endophora (internal textual references) and correctly answering precise
point-queries.
• Question and Answer (Q&A) Pairs: The next tier of granularity. A Q&A
pair may be derived from a single text chunk, or conversely, a question may arise
from one passage while the comprehensive answer requires spanning and integrating
information across multiple separate passages or chapters.
• Abstractive Summarization: This artifact operates across a wide spectrum of
scales. Summarization can occur at the level of a single chunk, a larger section,
a complete chapter, a full research paper, or entirely across the macro-level of the
whole corpus.
The realization that abstractive summarization can be executed at differing granular-
ities is the key insight that acts as the doorway to advanced architectures like RAPTOR
(Recursive Abstractive Processing for Tree-Organized Retrieval).
6.2
Scale-Dependent Phenomena in Physics and Knowledge
To understand why multiple levels of granularity are strictly necessary, one must look to
the physical sciences. Physicist Philip W. Anderson established in his influential paper,
More is Different, that distinct emergent phenomena only appear at different scales.
Consider boiling water in a household electric hot pot or kettle. At that localized
scale, the only visible phenomenon is steam rising. Boiling the water continuously simply
yields more steam. However, when the sun boils the Earth’s oceans, rivers, and lakes
at a massive planetary scale, the rising vapors create entirely new emergent physical
phenomena: clouds, thunderstorms, hurricanes, rainbows, snowflakes, and dew. A person
looking at the steam escaping a small kettle will never observe a snowflake or a rainbow;
those structures inherently require massive scale and altitude to manifest in nature.
The exact same principle governs information and knowledge corpuses. Knowledge
emerges differently at different scales.

Supervised learning splits into Classification and Regression. Unsupervised learning splits
into Clustering and Dimensionality Reduction. Within Classification exists a vast “zoo”

Topics inherently exist within topics, forming a strict hierarchical structure.
Any

The Information Bottleneck and Collective Wisdom
9.1
Outliers and the Bottleneck Principle
When analyzing an embedding space, not every vector fits neatly into a semantic cluster.
Outliers and noise exist continuously. For example, a text chunk reading “Copyright
2026 PQZ Publisher, All rights reserved” embedded in a machine learning textbook is an
outlier; it has no semantic relevance to the clusters governing neural networks. However,
within an overarching enterprise corpus (e.g., semiconductor chip design), all clusters,
even those with outliers, can eventually be summarized into the root node because the
implicit definition of the enterprise frames the context.
The true power of summarization in this architecture is explained by the Bottleneck
Principle. This phenomenon is universally experienced in classical literature classes. A
student may read a chapter of a story and thoroughly enjoy the narrative flow. How-
ever, the true test of comprehension occurs when the teacher walks into the classroom
and mandates that the entire chapter be summarized onto a single page. Furthermore,
if a student skipped reading the chapter entirely because watching a soccer game was
more appealing, this summarization mandate creates a forced information bottleneck.
The student is suddenly forced to read and deeply comprehend the text to execute the
summary.
By forcing the distillation of the text into a confined space, the brain, or
the LLM, must actively rank the data, separating the essential core narrative from the
non-essential peripheral details. This constraint mathematically maximizes the mutual
information between the full chapter and the single page.
Any summarization acts as an information bottleneck. By forcing an LLM to sum-
marize a cluster of text, it inherently ranks the data, prioritizing essential structural
knowledge while dropping extraneous details. The unavoidable, converse reality of any
summarization is the explicit loss of minute detail.

System Implementation and Multi-Index Strategies
10.1
Practical Architecture Execution with VLMs
To execute this architecture on a dense technical corpus, such as Bishop’s PRML text-
book, the workflow must move beyond basic parsing, chunking, and embedding.
First, generate the derivative artifacts explicitly from the text chunks: Factoids and
Q&A pairs. While the questions are generated at the chunk level, the derived answers
may span multiple chunks or pages. Following this, execute the RAPTOR-like abstractive
clustering and summarization to build the hierarchical tree.
However, for highly technical documentation, text-only parsing is insufficient. The
architecture must integrate Vision Language Models (VLMs). The VLM scans each indi-
vidual page of the corpus and generates a comprehensive textual explanation that fuses
the structural text with the visual diagrams, figures, and graphs. Instructing the VLM
with a strict rubric to explain the page from various perspectives yields an explanation
that incorporates the entire page structure, creating an incredibly rich derivative artifact.
10.2
Strict Index Separation
Once the raw chunks, factoids, Q&A pairs, VLMs explanations, and hierarchical sum-
maries are generated, a critical question arises: should they all reside in the same vector
database index?
The strict architectural recommendation is that they must be placed in different,
parallel indices. These artifacts are structurally and fundamentally different entities.
To use an agricultural analogy: If you live on a farm, you do not put the cattle, the
dogs, and the chickens to sleep in the exact same location. Because they are structurally
different beasts with different needs, they are separated out into different areas.
Similarly, the raw text embeddings and the derivative artifact embeddings must reside
in separate indices. When a query is initiated, the search engine searches all of these
indices in parallel, gathers the disparate results, and relies on an intelligent merging
mechanism to construct the final response.
It is inherently impossible to predict exactly what a user will ask, which creates a basic
aleatory concern. Because you cannot know in advance whether a query (e.g., “Why is
an order 15 polynomial likely to overfit on a small dataset?”) is best answered by the
raw text description of an oscillating graph, a precise factoid, a synthesized Q&A pair, or
a macro-summary, maintaining all variations in parallel indices ensures the RAG system
provides absolute, comprehensive search results.

it easy to miss approaching deal expirations.
11.5.2
System Pipeline & Multi-Modal Execution
The pipeline extracts, structures, and cross-references commercial promotions using the
following workflow:
• Extraction Layer: Connects to the user’s inbox, captures promotional emails,
and renders them as unified PDF or image snapshots.
• Multi-Modal Structuring: Processes these visual snapshots through a Vision-
Language Model (VLM) to extract embedded attributes, including brand iden-
tity, price thresholds, expiration dates, and conditional values.
• Historical Evaluation Engine: Aggregates and checks these offers against a
historical baseline of past promotional cycles. This allows the system to determine
if an offer (e.g., 75% off ) represents a genuine discount or a regular recurring
baseline.
• Query Capabilities: Allows users to query their promotions using flexible, time-
sensitive parameters (e.g., “Show me shoe deals under $80 expiring this week”).

11.7
Avaloka AI
11.7.1
Balancing Technical Accuracy and Compassionate Support
Standard automated chat tools often respond to users experiencing emotional distress
with dry, overly technical, or potentially unsafe advice. Avaloka addresses this chal-
lenge by anchoring its RAG pipeline in classical philosophical frameworks (such as core
Buddhist tenets centered on non-violence and compassion) combined with modern West-
ern psychological counseling methodologies.
11.7.2
Safety Architecture & Multi-Stage Processing
The system uses a multi-layered guardrail pipeline to ensure responses are consistently
safe, helpful, and constructive:
• Emotional Classification: Routes queries through an initial classification layer to
identify underlying emotional themes (e.g., fear of aging, isolation, or acute stress).
• SAGE Memory Architecture: Uses a specialized memory pattern that splits
operations into separate Memory Writers and Memory Readers. This ensures
that sensitive user context is handled safely while optimizing historical retrieval
speeds.
• Three-Layer Guardrail System: Evaluates all generated responses against strict
safety metrics to verify they remain completely harmless, helpful, and honest.
• Narrative Isolation Framework: Analyzes user queries to separate objective
real-world facts from the compounding internal narratives generated by stress or
anxiety. The system focuses on delivering practical, real-world grounding techniques
first, while maintaining safe boundaries by recommending medical consultation if
symptoms persist.
