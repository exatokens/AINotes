1
The Paradigm Shift in Information Retrieval
1.1
The Transmission of Human Knowledge
The survival and advancement of the human species rely entirely on the transmission
of knowledge from one generation to the next. Unlike lions or tigers, humans do not
possess biological defenses such as fangs or claws; humanity inherits knowledge.
As
the volume of knowledge grows, the relevant segments of that knowledge must remain
accessible. Whether stored as drawings on a cave wall, within the scrolls of the Library
of Alexandria, among the archives of the Library of Congress, or across the great digital
library of the World Wide Web, knowledge is practically non-existent if it cannot be
retrieved when needed. Therefore, search architectures hold a uniquely profound place
in the development of civilization.
1.2
From Library to Librarian
Before 2023, the search industry operated fundamentally like a vast library index. Whether
searching the open internet or an internal corporate intranet via systems like Elastic
Search or Solr, the modality remained the same. A query composed of keywords was
entered, and the system returned a list of URLs. The responsibility of drilling down
into those URLs, reading the material, understanding it, and manually reconstructing an
answer fell entirely on the user.
With the mass adoption of Large Language Models (LLMs), a fundamental shift
occurred. Language models possess the ability to understand human language, raising a
critical question: why shouldn’t these models read the underlying documents, parse the
web pages or videos, and figure out the answers directly? Fusing retrieval architectures

1.3
The Hallucination Paradox and Statistical Priors
Despite their fluency, LLMs face a fundamental limitation technically known as hallu-
cination. When a language model is asked a direct question, for example, “What did
I have for breakfast today?”, it utilizes a statistical prior. By ingesting all of human
literature and accumulated knowledge, the model recognizes patterns. It might deduce
that individuals in a specific demographic frequently consume coffee and bagels. Based
on the observation of a coffee pot in a breakroom, guessing “coffee” is absolutely logical.
The resulting answer is highly plausible, confidently delivered, and yet entirely in-
correct for that specific day, except by sheer accident. When a user queries a language
model, the safe presumption is that the user does not know the answer. The paradox and
danger arise when the model confidently presents a statistically plausible “prior belief”
as an absolute truth.
1.4
Retrieval-Augmented Generation as a Bayesian Machine
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) fundamentally solves the hallucination paradox
by operating as a Bayesian machine. In a Bayesian framework, a system moves from
a prior belief to a highly accurate posterior belief through the introduction of specific
evidence.
While the language model brings its prior beliefs encoded within its parametric mem-
ory (its weights and biases), a search engine intervenes by providing a crucial piece of
evidence, a retrieved document explicitly stating, for instance, that the specific breakfast
consisted of Indian bread, coffee, and pickles. By incorporating this precise evidence, the
language model is steered away from the generalized answer it would have given other-
wise. It incorporates the new data to form a highly accurate posterior belief, delivering
a factual, contextually grounded response.

practicalities and exigencies of their time. Software notoriously goes through iterations:
version one is when it might work, version two is when it begins to work, and version
three is when it actually functions. However, there is no “version two” of the Pythagorean

2.2
Mapping Thought to a Hyper-Globe
Text written in any human language, English, Spanish, Mandarin, Tamil, Hindi, or
French, must be converted into “Machinese,” the native tongue of artificial intelligence.
In Machinese, every thought or piece of text is translated into a coordinate point on a
high-dimensional hyper-globe.
When a text is processed, the language model translates it into a geographical loca-
tion on this mathematical structure. For instance, a document discussing physics might
map to a region equivalent to “Australia” on this globe, while a biological text maps
to “Europe,” and another concept lands in “Uganda.” This spatial placement dictates
semantic embedding: the geometrical location speaks directly to the semantic content of
the text. Models are explicitly designed to ensure that similar thoughts are kept strictly
in nearby neighborhoods.
2.3
The Necessity of High Dimensions
To differentiate simple concepts, low dimensions suffice. To separate a cow from a duck,
a two-dimensional grid plotting longitude for weight against latitude for volume works
perfectly.
Starting from a zero-weight, zero-volume South Pole, cows and ducks will
naturally map to entirely different locations.
However, a cow and a sofa are both heavy and voluminous. To prevent the machine
from confusing a piece of furniture with a bovine, a critical distinction, as sitting on a
cow is an unpleasant experience, whereas water buffalo are friendly enough for a child to
sleep on, additional dimensions are required to capture nuanced traits.
Human words are massive compressions of data. The single word “cow” carries an
immense semantic payload: its physical size, eating habits, gentle eyes, swishing tail,
milk production, and calves. Certain cultural texts, such as Indian Mahavakyas (great
sayings), mantras, or ayats, contain such dense semantic compression that unpacking
their full meaning requires a lifetime of study. To capture all the texture and nuances of
these compressions, extremely high-dimensional spaces are mandatory.
2.4
Engineering Constraints and Lossy Compression
Despite the need for high dimensionality, machines cannot utilize infinite dimensions.
Representing the simple sentence “The cow jumped over the moon” with a 1-million-
dimensional vector would require 4 million bytes (at 32-bit floating-point precision) for

a single thought. This creates a compute and storage catastrophe, as calculating the
distance (e.g., cosine dissimilarity or Euclidean distance) between points would become
overwhelmingly expensive.
Consequently, translating human language to embeddings is a “lossy” semantic com-
pression. It is not as perfectly lossless as an optical fiber transmitting error-corrected
bits and bytes, but it avoids the cascading errors found in the children’s game of “Tele-
phone.” Models compromise by representing text in a reasonable number of dimensions
that capture most of the meaning.
2.5
The Curse of Dimensionality and Concentration of Measure
High-dimensional spaces also suffer from the “Curse of Dimensionality.” In vast dimen-
sional spaces, everything is drawn incredibly far apart. The concept of a neighborhood
becomes extremely weak. It is akin to living in the rural countryside of Wyoming, where
speaking to a neighbor requires a one-hour drive in a truck, compared to the Bay Area,
where one can simply knock on a shared wall. Randomly distributed points will inherently
be distant from one another.
However, this high dimensionality offers a mathematical blessing known as the Con-
centration of Measure. If a specific data point acts as a “polar bear” sitting at the North
Pole, random vectors are naturally repelled away from the pole, congregating in an equa-
torial band. Therefore, if the polar bear manages to find any neighboring points near the
Arctic Circle, it is a certainty that those points are not there by random chance. They
are deeply related semantically.
This purity of signal is the singular breakthrough that revolutionized the search in-
dustry between 2018 and 2020. Semantic search is fundamentally AI search: it operates
on the profound geometric principle that proximity in high-dimensional space equates
directly to meaningful answers.

The Mechanics of Machine Learning: Probability, Surprise,
and Gradients
3.1
The Masked Language Modeling Game
The mechanism by which machines understand the nuances of language and determine
where to place points on the globe can be illustrated through a masked language modeling
exercise.
The core mechanics of this predictive challenge are structured as a card game titled
“Guess the Missing Word: A card game in three languages.”
The methodology for playing is precise:

• One sentence is presented per card, with exactly one word missing. The missing
word is always one of the 10 vocabulary words available in that deck.
• The player is given 10 chips (or dimes) per card. These chips must be distributed
over the 10 words based entirely on the player’s internal belief of what the correct
answer is. The probability p assigned to any word is the number of chips divided
by 10.
• At the reveal, the performance is evaluated using a metric called “surprise,” calcu-
lated as −log2(p) based only on the chips placed on the correct word.
• The lowest total surprise wins across three sequential decks: Tibetan, Spanish, and
English.
• Crucially, if zero chips are placed on the absolute truth, the calculation becomes
−log2(0), resulting in infinite surprise (∞). In this scenario, the player loses on the
spot.
Figure 1: Rules for the game: “Guess the Missing Word: A card game in three languages.”
The mathematical distribution of this game cannot be manipulated. The chip split
that minimizes the expected surprise represents the true belief. The surprise table outlines
the exact penalty for uncertainty:
• Placing all 10 chips on the correct word yields 0.00 bits of surprise.
• A completely random guess across all 10 words (1 chip each) establishes a “clueless
baseline” of 3.32 bits.
• Progressive confidence (e.g., 5 chips yielding 1.00 bit, 8 chips yielding 0.32 bits)
mathematically rewards accuracy.

3.3
The Bonus Round: Unidirectional vs. Bidirectional Vision
An important architectural distinction becomes apparent during advanced rounds of this
exercise. If a sentence is truncated and a predictive system is only permitted to see the
text to the left of a blank, its logic is constrained. For instance, given only the words “I
poured...”, the most statistically plausible guesses might be “tea” or “coffee.”
However, if the system is allowed to see the full text, both to the left and to the
right of the blank, the correct answer may completely change to “book.” A machine
that possesses bidirectional vision, capable of viewing the complete surround-
ing context, is fundamentally more powerful at resolving ambiguity than a
machine restricted to unidirectional, left-to-right vision.
3.4
Loss Functions and Aleatoric Uncertainty
In machine learning, this aggregate “Surprise” metric is formally known as a Loss Func-
tion.
It is a singular, powerful number indicating how poorly a system understands
a language. Imagine an entity, whether an algorithm or a human in a Chinese room,
hidden behind a curtain, repeatedly attempting to predict missing words. If the entity
continually generates infinite net surprise, it possesses no understanding. If the surprise
progressively decreases over successive attempts, transitioning from high numbers down
to near zero, it is empirical evidence that the entity is learning statistical patterns and
comprehending the language.

Sometimes, surprise cannot reach absolute zero due to Aleatoric Uncertainty, an

For instance, predicting whether a

Similarly, in an enterprise RAG system, while the broad domain (e.g., semiconductors) is

3.5
Optimization Through Gradient Descent
To actively learn, a machine must evaluate its predictions (ˆY , pronounced “Y hat”)
against the correct answers, known as the ground truth or target (Y ). The machine
focuses on its most egregious mistakes, the guesses that generated the highest surprise,
using them as a guide.
To improve, the machine computes the Gradient, which is the sensitivity of the loss
to the machine’s internal dials (parameters). By understanding exactly how turning a
specific dial left or right impacts the overall loss, the machine systematically adjusts its
parameters to drive the error downward. This optimization process is known as gradient
descent, the core technique that enables machine learning models to transition from
profound ignorance to high-level language comprehension.

Deep Bidirectional Transformers and Text Embeddings
4.1
The BERT Architecture
The classroom guessing game directly mirrors the fundamental mechanics of Masked Lan-
guage Modeling (MLM), the architecture that powers landmark AI models. This concept
was formalized in a seminal research paper titled “BERT: Pre-training of Deep Bidirec-
tional Transformers for Language Understanding,” authored by researchers at Google AI
Language [Devlin et al., 2018].
The abstract of the paper defines BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from
Transformers) as a novel language representation model designed to pre-train deep repre-
sentations from unlabeled text by jointly conditioning on both the left and right context
in all layers.
This bidirectional capability is paramount. Older causal transformers could only look
to the past (the left of a blank space) to predict the missing word. However, a ma-
chine’s predictive power increases exponentially when it has bidirectional vision, allowing
it to look at the text both preceding and following a mask to derive precise meaning.
BERT proved empirically powerful, pushing the state-of-the-art results on eleven natural
language processing benchmarks, including GLUE and MultiNLI.

4.2
Pre-training vs. Fine-Tuning (Transfer Learning)
The AI industry relies on the principle of Transfer Learning, driven by neural scaling
laws. Generally, the larger the model and the more data it is fed, the more capable it
becomes. However, training massive models requires compute budgets in the millions,
resources restricted to hyperscalers and massive tech corporations.
The solution is split into two phases:
1. Pre-training: Hyperscalers utilize surrogate tasks, such as Masked Language Mod-
eling. Taking vast amounts of scraped, unlabeled web data, the system deliberately
masks words and forces the massive neural network to guess them, looping through
gradient descent until the loss function minimizes. While building a “fill-in-the-
blank” application has no immediate business utility, the surrogate task successfully
imbues the model with a core, foundational understanding of human language.
2. Fine-tuning: Practitioners take these pre-trained, open-weight models and adapt
them to highly specific downstream tasks. Because the pre-trained model already
speaks fluent “Machinese,” it requires only a few additional output layers and min-
imal task-specific data to become a state-of-the-art classifier (e.g., analyzing cus-
tomer reviews) or a question-answering system.

4.3
Pooling: Distilling Context into Text Embeddings
When a sentence enters an attention-based transformer network, every single word ac-
tively searches for its full meaning. Due to polysemy (words having multiple meanings),
a word like “bank” will mathematically analyze surrounding words like “river,” “swam,”
and “across” to pull context, emerging as a fully contextualized vector.
However, an engineering challenge arises: how does a system represent an entire multi-
word sentence as a single coordinate point (a Text Embedding) on the hyper-globe? This
reduction process is called Pooling, meaning creating one out of many.
Figure 6: Pooling illustration.
The architecture diagrams outline two primary methods for pooling:
1. The [CLS] Token (The Elected Representative): A special, inherently mean-
ingless classification token ([CLS]) is prepended to the beginning of the text, along-
side a separator token ([SEP]). Because [CLS] holds no innate dictionary meaning,
it spends the entire processing cycle aggressively soaking up the context of every
other word in the sentence. When it emerges from the network, it serves as the text
embedding, an “elected spokesperson” or congressman that perfectly captures the
overall semantic meaning of the sentence.
2. Mean Pooling (The Collective Average): Alternatively, the system can math-
ematically aggregate all the contextualized vectors of every word in the sentence
and divide by N (the total number of tokens). This calculates the precise average
mathematical meaning of the entire thought. Over time, subsequent research has
demonstrated that Mean Pooling generally provides a superior, more robust text
embedding.
Once this pooled text embedding is generated, it serves as the input vector for any
downstream machine learning algorithm, allowing systems to easily compute entailment,
contradictions, or direct question-answer relevance.

Continuous Learning and the Future of Knowledge Architec-
tures
5.1
Derivative Artifacts
Standard out-of-the-box RAG implementations typically chunk and process raw docu-
ments directly. While suitable for simple proofs of concept, applying generic downloaded
models to raw text generally yields poorer retrieval quality and is insufficient for world-
class enterprise systems.
Advanced architectures construct Derivative Artifacts. In these systems, the AI
is programmed to invest computational effort to deeply analyze, cross-reference, and
understand the corpus before it ever answers a user query. This process of generating
derivative artifacts lays the foundation for highly accurate, agentic RAG systems.
5.2
Fluid Embeddings and Self-Evolving Agents
Human cognition involves continuous learning; the brain solidifies connections and deep-
ens understanding over time, even during sleep when no new external information is
actively flowing in. The ultimate trajectory of RAG architectures is to mimic this be-
havior through self-evolving agents.
In basic systems, a text string’s vector coordinates are fixed. In advanced, continuous-
learning architectures, Fluid Embeddings are utilized. As the system processes data
and evaluates connections, its internal understanding evolves.
If the system initially

The Foundations of Probability, Surprise, and Negative Log
Likelihood
6.1
The Language Game and Probability Sand
The post-lunch session opened with a reflection on the morning’s language exercise, where
the class played a “fill in the blanks” game using Tibetan, Spanish, and English sentences.
Two primary human behaviors emerged from this exercise:
1. Accumulating Surprise: When students didn’t know Tibetan, they accumulated
a massive amount of “surprise.” Conversely, for the English sentences, the surprise
was essentially zero.
2. Spreading vs. Snapping: When uncertain, humans instinctively “hedge their
bets,” spreading their guesses (their conceptual “dimes”) across multiple potential
words. When confident in English, humans piled all their chips onto a single correct
word.
This human intuition maps perfectly to how machine learning models operate. When
an LLM predicts the next word, it generates a probability mass distribution. Think
of probability as a bucket of sand. You pour different amounts of sand into bowls repre-
senting possible words. Because you have exactly one bucket of sand, this introduces the
Law of Conservation of Probability: The sum of all probabilities assigned across all
possible outcomes must strictly equal 1.
6.2
The Mathematical Definition of Surprise
Let us introduce the emotion of “surprise” as a mathematical variable. If a student holds
a prior belief that there is a 0% chance of rain, and it rains, their surprise is infinite. If
they believe there is an 80% chance of rain, their surprise is very low. Thus, surprise and
probability have an inverse relationship.
The class brainstormed mathematical formulas for surprise:
• 1/p: This fails because if p = 1 (100% certainty), the surprise is 1, but it should
intuitively be 0.
• (1/p) −1: This works mathematically, but there is a deeper, more elegant solution.

6.3
Negative Log Likelihood (NLL)
When a machine makes predictions across a batch of 32 independent sentences, its success
is measured by the joint probability (the likelihood) of getting all answers correct. The
joint probability of independent events is their product (P1 × P2 × P3 . . . P32).
If you ask a computer to multiply 32 small probabilities (e.g., numbers between 0 and
1), the result quickly hits numerical underflow (e.g., 8 × 10−15). Computers cannot
16

process numbers this infinitesimally small; they round them to zero, making optimization
impossible.
The logarithm acts as a magician, converting multiplication into addition:
log(P1 × P2 . . . ) = log(P1) + log(P2) . . .
(2)
By taking the log, these numbers become manageable negative sums (e.g., −33.27).
To train a model to *maximize* the log likelihood (Maximum Likelihood Estimator,
MLE), we simply tell optimization algorithms to *minimize* the negative of that sum.

Logits, Softmax, and Temperature
7.1
Logits and The Stadium Analogy
Machine learning models output raw, unbounded numbers called logits, which can range
from −∞to +∞. For example, when fans watch a player, they cheer (positive numbers)
or boo (negative numbers). Similarly, if a machine is classifying an image of an animal
among 10 options, it might output a logit of −65 for “zebra” (strong disbelief) and +100
for “duck” (strong belief).
However, logits are not probabilities. To convert logits into a probability distribution,
we use the Softmax function—referred to as the “Cheshire Cat” of machine learning
because it reliably appears across all deep learning architectures.

magnitude order (e.g., −2 remains larger than −3) but converts all outputs to

7.4
Sampling the Softmax and Determinism
A neural network is at the end of the day a mathematical function.
A function by
definition should produce exactly the same output for the same input over and over
again. If you wrote a square root function and every time you gave it the input 9 it
sometimes produced 3, sometimes 4, sometimes 2—that would not be a good function.
So a neural network is inherently deterministic.
But language models don’t behave that way. Every time you ask the same question,
you get a different answer lexically, though the semantic core remains stable.
Why
would people destroy determinism? Because they noticed that without something called
sampling, the model generated boring and repetitive answers. It would get into a loop
and keep repeating itself. In the early days of ChatGPT 3.5, if you asked something very
complex, it would sometimes start repeating sentences.
The intuition behind sampling is this: imagine there is probability sand lying in
bowls, and you send a kid in a hurry to quickly pick the bowl with the most sand. The
kid is likely to pick up the heaviest bowl, but sometimes—especially when the differences
between bowls are small (high temperature)—the kid might grab the second or third
heaviest instead. That is sampling. You’re more likely to pick the highest-mass item, but
the kid may pick any one of them.
Once a token is sampled, it commits the model to that path. If the question is “I
have $10, ice cream costs $4 and pizza costs $2, should I have ice cream?” and the first
sampled token is “Yes,” the model now has to justify that answer. If instead it had
sampled “No,” it would have justified the opposite. That is how variability comes into
the machine.
If you don’t want sampling, that is called the greedy approach—you turn off sam-
pling, and it basically becomes hardmax. You can set the temperature to zero, which
turns off sampling altogether, but generally that’s not considered a good idea unless you
are in a very strict domain like legal.
Temperature as the creativity knob: The higher the temperature, the more
likely the model is to go in unexpected directions. The lower the temperature, the more
it behaves like a deterministic machine.
7.4.1
The Temperature Paradox in RAG
Student’s question: “If I build a RAG system, I would want it to give the same answer
over and over again. Why would I leave sampling on? Why would I leave temperature
at, say, 1?”
The instructor posed this as a paradox: if you ask “How many days of PTO can I
take?” in an HR application, and one day the system says “I think 40 is just about right
for you” and another day says “No, you’re only allowed five”—that would be a disaster.

And yet, setting temperature to zero is usually not a very good idea.
The answer is nuanced. For point-answer situations (like the PTO question), yes, you
can set it low. But generally, wherever reasoning, thinking, or evaluation is involved,
it is not a good idea. In fact, when GPT-5 first came out, OpenAI explicitly would
not let you set the temperature below 1. It was a reasoning model—its job was to tell
you the answer to math problems—and yet they forced temperature to 1. If you tried
temperature zero, it would bail on you. (GPT 5.2 onwards they changed it back and now
you can set different temperature values.)
Why would a company do that? Because these language models are not just next-
token prediction machines. They are complex machines that internally have concepts.
There is a paper from Anthropic on polysemanticity showing that if you open up the lay-
ers, you can see concepts forming. There is another paper from Apple Research showing
that when the model predicts a token—say it produces “cow” in “the cow jumped over
the moon”—if you look at the probability sand that accumulated on the word “jumped,”
it is already rising even though the model hasn’t produced it yet. The machine is already
thinking through the whole sentence and producing it one by one. This whole movement
is called mechanistic interpretability, and it remains a deep, active area of research.
7.4.2
Lexical Variance vs. Semantic Consistency
To demonstrate the distinction, the instructor ran a classroom experiment.
He said:
“Yesterday evening, after a hot day, from the Pacific, fog came in and rolled over the San
Francisco hills.” Then he asked students to repeat the same thought in their own words:
• Student A said: “Fog came in and rolled over the area.”
• Student B said: “San Francisco Bay area, fog rolled over the hills.”
Two human beings expressed the same semantic content using completely different
words.
LLMs behave exactly the same way—they preserve very stably the semantic
content, but on repeated asking of the same question, the specific word constructions
change while what they mean remains the same.
Even for “2+2,” the answer is always semantically “four,” but the model might say
“Four,” or “Four is a number,” or “The sum is four,” or “It adds up to four.”
7.4.3
Hardware Indeterminacy
It is a common misconception that if you set the temperature to zero, you get a fully
deterministic machine. Actually, you don’t. These models are ginormous. They run
on big GPUs. Those GPUs share their weights, and their numerical rounding issues,
floating-point precision limits, and the sequence in which the answers arrive for matrix

Don’t ever set the temperature to zero in your RAG systems and then assume your

7.5
Theory of the Mind in LLMs
If you ask an LLM “What is 2+2?” it just says “4.” But the instructor posed a different
prompt: “I’m a number theorist. What does it mean to say 2+2 is 4?” The model’s
answer changed drastically—it began discussing abstraction, deep philosophers of science
and mathematics like Bertrand Russell and Whitehead who laid the foundation of ax-
iomatic mathematics. It dwelt upon what it means to define “1,” to define “2,” what
“equal to” means. It noted that in binary arithmetic there is no four, there is no two,
and that in modular 3 arithmetic 2+2 is not four—it is one. There are many implicit
assumptions, and if you change the context, the model answers differently.
This is because machines have a Theory of the Mind. When we talk to each other,
we have a theory of what the other person knows and will understand, and we answer
contextually. Theory of the mind is certainly not just the prerogative of humans; it has
been observed in most animals and was recently discovered even among fishes.
To the great surprise of researchers, language models turned out to have a theory
of the mind too. Based on what they think the user is and how the user thinks, they
calibrate their response.
This complicates matters for RAG—if you ask “How many
days of leave am I allowed?” and the model has the ability to infer who you are, will
the answer change? The answer is: the semantic core will remain the same for factual
queries. Assuming the search engine has retrieved the right document with the leave
table at your level of position and title, it will still give the same answer. Its expressions
may change—its language may vary—but the semantic core will remain the same. You
don’t have to worry that because temperature is not zero, the answer will suddenly go
from 4 days to 40 days.

The Dictionary Lookup Analogy
The first exercise: Suppose you receive the garbled word “receiv.” What does it remind

But what made you do that? You don’t judge solely by lexical string distance. In

The language models have studied the world literature and seen all the misspellings. How

something interesting happens: the string distance (a simple transposition of one letter)
is identical for both. Traditional string distance can’t help you. But contextual statistics
from vast human literature tell you that “the” is overwhelmingly more likely—roughly
85%. The machine understands that “teh” is semantically closer to “the.” This is the
distinction between pure lexical distance and a semantic scoring based on experience.
Example 4: “ruinng.” Candidates are “running,” “done,” and “ruin.” Weights:
85%, 10%, 5%. Once again, the statistics of human error inform the machine’s attention.
You would say the meaning of this garbled word is “running” with a touch of “done” and
spiced up with “ruin” a tiny bit. You can never be sure that it wasn’t “ruin.”

The Spectrum of Meaning: Where Does It Live?
There is a critical spectrum for understanding where a word’s meaning resides:
1. Meaning fully in the word. If someone says “plier,” immediately a mental image
of the tool comes up. A lot of the meaning is right there in the word itself.
2. Meaning partly in the word, partly in the context. Polysemous words like
“bank” are classic examples. “Bank” has many meanings:
• “You banked on me to bring biryani for lunch.”
• “You deposit your salary in the bank.”
• “The airplane banks to land, to line up with the runway.”

8.2
The Attention Heat Map
Now consider the sentence: “She swam across the river to the other bank.”
If you make a square grid of words (she, swam, across, the, river, to, the, other, bank),
each word goes looking for its meaning. The first act of looking should be in the mirror—
because some meaning may be right there in the word itself. “Swam” might make its
meaning pretty clear. “River” has quite a bit of meaning in the word itself—it might
ascribe maximum attention to itself, and then some attention to other words to be sure
it’s still a flowing water body.
The result is a heat map of attention scores. You will get a pronounced diagonal
(each word pays strong attention to itself) but the word “bank” will be giving a lot of
attention to “river” and “swam”—those are relevant words that disambiguate its meaning.
“She” and “the” may not be so relevant.
To be able to create a high-fidelity, meaningful, correct heat map is the core of un-
derstanding text. If each word is completely unambiguous in what it means in context,
the machine has at that moment understood the sentence.

8.3
Machines Speak Machinese: Queries, Keys, and Values
So now the question comes: machines speak machines. What is the notion of score based
on proximity? If all points sit on a globe, the simplest proximity measure is the angle
between them—the smaller the angle, the more proximal. A measure of that is cosine.
But when the vectors are not equal length, it is the dot product—how aligned the two
vectors are. The score function of a query with respect to a key word is basically the dot
product between the query and the key.
But dot products are not normalized—they can be positive or negative. Attention
scores should be positive (you can’t give negative attention to anything—like in the hiking
example, where you give different amounts of attention to things in the forest, but those
scores are positive degrees of emphasis). So how do you take a bunch of dot products,
positivize them, and make them add up to one? You softmax it—exponentiate and

at that thing from the necessary lens.

8.5.1
Self-Attention: The Small Dictionary
When you have a sentence like “She swam across the river to the other bank,” the
word “bank” needs to figure out its meaning by looking at only these words. The only
dictionary it needs is a small dictionary of key-values made up of just the words in
the sentence. You don’t need the whole Oxford Learner’s Dictionary or any linguistic
dictionary, because the meaning has to be inferred from this text. That is the self in
self-attention: a word searches for its meaning from a small dictionary made out of only
the words in the text. The text is the context.
A student raised an important point: that self-attention assumes all these words
are correct. The instructor demonstrated by writing a made-up sentence: “The vu-
lat gravited downly but oer groed.” Every word is searching for its meaning and
scratching its head. He referenced Lewis Carroll’s *Jabberwocky*—a poem made entirely
of invented words that still feels like it makes sense when read aloud. He deliberately
did not use Jabberwocky as the class example because LLMs would immediately figure
it out—so much has been written about it that the model already has the context. So
he created a nonsense sentence of his own instead.
The limitation of self-attention: the context should be revealing enough. It mostly
works—until you come across Jabberwocky or its variant.

• The Agentic Loop: AI intelligence is defined by a continuous cycle of sensing,

• RAG Architecture: A robust pipeline decouples Search and Retrieval from the
