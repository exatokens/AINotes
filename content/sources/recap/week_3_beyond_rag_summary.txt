Foundations of Knowledge Architectures
Building effective knowledge architectures demands a geometric and probabilistic un-
derstanding of how machines reason about text. A geometric approach frames textual
meaning within an embedding space, allowing systems to quantify semantic relationships.
Central to this architecture is the softmax function, which governs token probabilities,
alongside foundational models that derive reasoning capabilities through multi-lingual
structural games involving languages such as Tibetan, Spanish, and English. The core
mechanisms of self-attention are derived from these first principles, integrating advanced
cognitive mappings like butterfly prompt decomposition to orchestrate complex reason-
ing.
A critical mechanism for refining these embeddings is the application of contrastive
loss. When a machine is trained using contrastive loss, it is guided by two opposing geo-
metric forces: a force of attraction between semantically similar items and a force of repul-
sion between dissimilar items. This dual-force mechanism directly mitigates anisotropy,
the tendency for embedding vectors to cluster narrowly. By reducing anisotropy, the
embedding space achieves a better spread and superior utilization of the hypersphere’s
surface. Different subjects float away from each other, resulting in a more diffused, highly
separated structure where the compactness of similar concepts is precisely controlled by
a mathematical temperature parameter.
1.1
The Fundamental Question: Why?
In any advanced retrieval system, the foundational step involves taking a raw document,
parsing it into a structured format such as Markdown or plain text, and breaking it down

into smaller, distinct segments. While this process, known as chunking, is widely utilized,
its underlying epistemology requires intense scrutiny. It is necessary to ask fundamental
questions: Why must documents be chunked? What are the structural consequences of
this division? Should the process be bypassed entirely in favor of emerging capabilities?
1.1.1
Primary Motivations
One core motivation for this division originates from principles of software engineering.
Just as programmers utilize hashing functions to differentiate discrete objects within a
system, chunking serves to separate textual concepts so they can be isolated, encoded, and
retrieved independently. Beyond cognitive structuring, a primary consideration revolves
around engineering constraints. Cloud computing providers charge based on token input,
creating a direct financial cost for processing massive documents. Furthermore, processing
vast amounts of text incurs substantial latency. While hardware-level mechanisms like
KV caching, which stores precomputed keys and values in the attention layers, partially
mitigate the quadratic compute costs during query response, the bandwidth required
to read and transport massive datasets across networks remains a severe bottleneck.
However, the deepest flaws in processing unchunked documents stem not from engineering
limitations, but from cognitive and mathematical degradation within the model itself.
1.1.2
The Deceptive Promise of the Infinite Context Window
A prevalent argument in modern technology discourse is that Retrieval-Augmented Gen-
eration is obsolete due to the advent of frontier models possessing massive context win-
dows capable of holding one to two million tokens. A cursory survey of video platforms
yields a flood of content declaring that “RAG is dead,” asserting that these vast context
windows have rendered retrieval architectures completely unnecessary. The assumption
posits that if an entire document fits within the context window, parsing and chunking
are unnecessary. This assumption is mathematically and practically flawed.
1.1.3
Attention Dilution and the Credit Card Analogy
In attention-based language models, every single token computes an attention weight
relative to every other token.
To understand the consequences of this, consider the
anatomy of a monthly credit card bill. At the end of a billing cycle, a massive total
balance is rarely the result of a single, catastrophic purchase. Instead, the balance is
accumulated through dozens of trifling, low-value micro-transactions, a coffee, a bus
ticket, routine library dues.
The softmax function within self-attention mechanisms operates identically. When
evaluating a specific, high-value token such as bank in the sentence She swam across
the river to the other bank, the model must assign heavy attention weights to highly

pertinent words like river, she, and across. However, the mathematical necessity of the
softmax equation dictates that the model must also assign fractional attention to every
other word in the provided text. When an unchunked, massive document is fed into the
context window, the model’s finite attention capacity is squandered on tens of thousands
of irrelevant, trifling tokens. This attention dilution drastically reduces the model’s ability
to focus on the strictly pertinent evidence.
1.1.4
Context Rot and the Density of Ambiguity
Human language possesses an inherent density of ambiguity. Practical wisdom dictates
that when speaking to legal authorities, one should be exceptionally brief, answering
only the precise question asked. Volunteering excess information introduces unnecessary
ambiguity, potentially framing guilt through contradictory statements regardless of actual
innocence.
Providing a language model with an entire, massive document introduces the exact
same vulnerability. Expanding the text volume expands the surface area for contradic-
tions, irrelevant tangents, and contextual rot. Historically, this manifested as the Lost in
the Middle phenomenon. Human readers naturally remember the opening lines of a text,
such as the famous Hello World introduction in the classic The C Programming Language
textbook Kernighan and Ritchie [1988], and the climactic unraveling of a mystery novel’s
ending. However, the contents of page 100 are invariably forgotten. Language models
exhibit an identical degradation, severely struggling to retrieve precise facts buried deep
within a bloated, unchunked context window.
1.2
Granularity and Context Engineering
Determining exactly what information to feed an intelligent system requires distinguishing
between the fundamental types of user queries and understanding the physical limits of
enterprise data.
1.2.1
Point Queries vs. Evaluative Queries
Queries exist on a spectrum of granularity. A point query demands a strictly atomic
fact: What is the capital of Germany, and what is its population? If a user requires this
specific data, handing them all 26 volumes of the Encyclopedia Britannica is actively
unhelpful. Common sense dictates locating the exact volume, chapter, and paragraph
detailing Berlin’s demographics and providing only that specific, high-fidelity snippet.
Conversely, evaluative queries contain aleatoric uncertainty, a fundamental ambiguity
regarding the required granularity of the answer. Such queries require sensemaking over
an entire corpus. Consider a narrative adaptation of Dostoevsky’s Crime and Punish-
ment Dostoevsky [1866], modernized to feature a CEO of a high-flying AI company, one

1.2.2
The Library of Congress Paradigm
Humanity’s data volume is currently experiencing explosive, exponential growth, dou-
bling approximately every two years. While frontier research, such as DeepSeek’s En-
gram (EnM) paper DeepSeek-AI [2026], explores the possibility of utilizing extra mem-
ory as a sidecar to inject facts directly into a model without expanding the base window,
the underlying hardware capabilities and primary context windows still scale at a sub-
exponential, linear rate. It is physically impossible to fit an entire enterprise corpus,
analogous to the Library of Congress, into any single context window. Because a com-
plete corpus cannot be ingested simultaneously, retrieval architectures must extract and
supply highly specific subsets of that data.
1.2.3
The Knapsack Problem
The context window functions as a highly constrained knapsack. If a user asks a complex
question regarding the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution, the system cannot load an entire
thermodynamics library into the knapsack. It must identify and extract only the most
precise, pertinent paragraphs across multiple physics texts.
This process is the essence of context engineering. From a Bayesian perspective, the
language model approaches a query with a baseline prior belief. The curated, high-quality
facts placed within the knapsack serve as the critical evidence. Synthesizing this injected
evidence with the model’s intrinsic capabilities produces the most accurate posterior
output.

The Epistemology of Chunking
If the context window must be curated with extreme precision, the central architectural
challenge becomes identifying the exact boundary, or size, of a thought.
2.1
Too Large Chunks: Linearization and The Centroid Delusion
Written text inherently forces complex, multi-dimensional concepts into a linearized,
sequential format. An author chains Thought A to Thought B, progresses to Thought C,
and may later loop back to a related Thought A’.
Figure 1: Illustration of Linearization and Centroid Delusion
If distinct thoughts map to pure, specific points on the surface of an embedding hyper-
sphere, applying arbitrary, block-based chunking creates severe mathematical distortions.
Consider a single text chunk containing three completely unrelated facts:
1. The cow jumped over the moon.
2. Nvidia stocks crashed today.
3. Today is a good day to go to the beach.

Embedding each sentence individually yields three highly distinct vector coordinates.
However, concatenating these strings into a single chunk and passing it through an em-
bedding model forces the system to calculate an arithmetic centroid location between the
three points. The resulting embedding vector represents an averaged, middle ground that
fails to accurately capture the semantic truth of any individual thought. This mathemat-
ical homogenization is known as the Centroid Delusion, rendering the resulting vector
utterly useless for precise semantic retrieval.
2.2
Arbitrary or Too Small Chunks
Attempting to avoid the Centroid Delusion by creating highly granular, small chunks in-
troduces devastating linguistic fragmentation. Human language relies on invisible struc-
tural dependencies that break down entirely when text is blindly sliced.
2.2.1
Endophora: Severing Grammatical Anchors
Consider the sequence: Sally swam across the river. Then she ate her breakfast.
If a chunk boundary severs these two sentences, the resulting isolated chunk, Then she
ate her breakfast, loses all semantic meaning. The pronoun she relies on an anaphoric
relationship, pointing backward to the anchor entity Sally. Alternatively, cataphora
occurs when a pronoun points forward to an entity introduced later in the text (She
swam... then Sally ate). Together, these mechanisms are known as Endophora.
When naive chunking algorithms blindly slice text, they routinely decapitate these
endophoric links. Similarly, textbooks frequently reference external anchors (for example:
Read this in conjunction with Equation 3.6), and academic papers point to bibliographies
at the far end of the document. Isolating these references dilutes the contextual fabric
essential for robust self-attention.

2.2.2
Rhetorical Structure Theory and Bridging Inferences
Documents are not mere sequences of characters; they are complex, interlocking webs
of relationships governed by Rhetorical Structure Theory. Sentences rely on unstated,
implicit logic known as bridging inferences. For instance: John walked into a room. The
chandelier was magnificent.
The text never explicitly states that the chandelier was
located on the ceiling of that specific room, yet the human mind instantly builds that
logical bridge. Separating these sentences via chunking destroys the capacity for models
to infer that unstated spatial relationship.
2.2.3
The Negation Trap and Scope Failure
Scope failures represent the most dangerous side effect of arbitrary chunking because they
do not simply lose information, they actively invert the truth. This occurs frequently
when cause-and-effect relationships or critical negations span across a text boundary.
Figure 3: Illustration of cause-and-effect relationships within text.
Consider a highly contextualized finding within a medical document:
The meta-analysis found no significant evidence that Compound X increases cardiac
risk in patients under 65. However, in patients 65-80 with hypertension, the data sug-
gested a concerning elevation.
If a retrieval algorithm splits this paragraph and surfaces only the second sentence, it
presents a stark, terrifying medical alarm. Conversely, if it surfaces only the first sentence,
all warnings vanish. The absolute scientific truth exists solely within the relationship
between the two sentences. Cutting the text at the boundary of that relationship actively
fabricates a falsehood.
2.2.4
Presupposition Accommodation
Academic and scientific writing frequently employs presupposition accommodation. A
researcher will often begin a paper by outlining a highly tentative, heavily caveated
hypothesis. However, to explore the logical boundaries of that hypothesis throughout the
remainder of the paper, the researcher cannot constantly reiterate the hedging language.

Getting Real: The Fallacy of Uniform Chunking
A fundamental structural failure preceding all parsing errors is Genre Blindness, the
catastrophic assumption that all documents share an identical information architecture.
3.1
Mixing Tribes and Private Languages
Different academic and professional disciplines operate as distinct tribes, each utilizing
a highly specialized private language.
This tribal differentiation mirrors the ancient
historical test of the shibboleth, where a group identified embedded spies by their inability
to pronounce the specific word shibboleth, the impostors calling it chibboleth instead. Just
as that single word separated people, modern textual tribes are separated by profound
structural shifts. A computer science professional wandering into a chemistry department

will find themselves lost amidst discussions of molecular formulas, despite both groups
speaking English. Beyond specialized vocabulary, the underlying rhetorical structure of
the text shifts radically:
• Novels utilize narrative arcs, building tension, mystery, and climactic resolutions.
• Legal Contracts represent the exact antithesis of narrative. Documents like Sec-
tion 5 of the European AI Act are written as hyper-dense, condensed code, designed
to eliminate all ambiguity through rigid, precise clauses.
• Scientific Papers follow strict logical progressions, universally adhering to the
IMRAD framework: Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion.
3.2
The 512-Token Fallacy
Despite these profound structural differences, the industry default for chunking text is fre-
quently hardcoded to an arbitrary 512 tokens. Converting raw words to tokens generally
follows a 4:3 ratio (accounting for prefixes, suffixes like ing, and punctuation marks). A
standard printed line contains 8 to 10 words, equating to roughly 12 tokens. A standard
page of 35 lines therefore contains approximately 420 tokens.
Setting a universal chunk size of 512 tokens equates to roughly one densely printed
page. While educational environments or foundational clustering labs might implement
arbitrary limits, such as mandating 300-token chunks for dense math and chemistry texts
simply because adopting a naive, hardcoded threshold is an expedient starting point where
a degree of procedural laziness is momentarily tolerated, deploying such rigid parameters
in a production environment is architecturally indefensible. Assuming that exactly one
pure, distinct thought exists within every single page of text, regardless of whether it is a
fast-paced news article or a dense legal statute, is a profound architectural fallacy. One
size does not fit all.
3.3
Connective Tissue and Anisotropy
When chunks are drawn too large, they inadvertently capture massive amounts of linguis-
tic connective tissue, the high-frequency words and standard English syntax necessary to
bind ideas together. While the core thoughts within two large chunks might be distinct,
the sheer volume of common syntax dominates the vector embedding. The similarities
drag the distinct points toward each other in the latent space, increasing anisotropy.
This blurring of boundaries causes search algorithms to return generic, highly popular,
but ultimately useless text segments rather than surgical, highly precise knowledge.

3.4
The Fragility of Semantic Meaning
The absolute necessity of intelligent, semantic text parsing over raw string manipulation
is proven by the extreme fragility of human language. A shift in structural meaning does
not require new vocabulary; it requires only the displacement of a single punctuation
mark.
Consider the grammatical punctuation test evaluating the title of a well-known book:
1. Eats, shoots and leaves.
2. Eats shoots, and leaves.
3. A panda eats bamboo shoots and leaves.
The first sentence, populated with commas, outlines a rapid sequence of aggressive
actions, instantly evoking the image of a gunslinger in a Western thriller. The second
sentence, containing the exact same vocabulary but stripped of commas, transforms the
verbs into nouns and direct objects, perfectly describing the dietary habits of a cute
panda bear.
Figure 5: Illustration of the three sentences for grammatical punctuation exercise.
To empirically prove how critical this structural nuance is, one must calculate the
cosine distances between these concepts within the embedding space. Evaluating the
pairwise cosine similarities, cos(1, 2), cos(1, 3), and cos(2, 3), is left as an exercise for the
students.
Ideally, (2) and (3) should be near, and (1) should be far apart. However, when
finding pair-wise correlations and pair-wise cosine similarities with embedding models on
these sentences, almost all of them got it wrong. Quite often, the embedding models
found that sentences one and two are much closer according to the model than sentences
two and three. That is a disaster. It shows that even today, June 2026, embedding
models have to be used with caution.

The Fundamental Challenge
4.1
The Half-a-Worm Problem
When building RAG systems, the instinct of people is to segment documents. However,
chunking inherently has problems and limitations. A classic analogy illustrates this prob-
lem: “What is worse than a worm in an apple? Half a worm.” When you bite into an
apple and see half a worm there, you have about the same reaction as when you bite into
documents and realize that it has split in the middle of a word or tokens. Fixed-size token
chunking would be fixed-size string characters chunking (e.g., 512 characters, a thousand
characters or something like that), but at least with tokens, you have something coming
up. However, it might chunk in the middle of a sentence, which will completely destroy
the semantic integrity of what was being said near the end or the beginning.
4.2
The Endophora Problem
Endophora represents a visual or textual nuance where text relies on other elements for
its meaning, such as saying “as you see in the figure above,” “as you see on the table on
the right,” or “as you see in the figure below.” When sentences are broken up blindly,
this context is lost.

Text RAG vs. Visual RAG
5.1
The Visual Page as a Semantic Unit
The structure of scientific textbooks in STEM fields is fundamentally multifaceted and
multimodal. A single page is dwelling upon one thought and jointly talking of a single
idea. A typical page contains four major components:
• Running text (prose)
• Figures/Images (e.g., an image of a coffee cup with steam/water vapors rising from
it)
• Plots (e.g., a Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution plot where the y-axis is probability
of energy and the x-axis is energy)
• Tables (e.g., a table of distributions’ mean, standard deviation, skewness, kurtosis,
moments, etc.)
• The physical position and layout of these components on the page
The entire page has to be jointly studied as one single semantic unit. It would be
wrong to say that the semantic content is just in the running prose. If you use a typical
OCR tool that just parses this page and extracts only the text out of it, it would lose a
lot. The extracted text gets out of context because the prose loses its context without the
right positioning of figures. More and more technical documents conform to this layout
structure rather than to running prose.

5.2
The PRML Experiment
A practical lab experiment was conducted using an open-access scientific textbook: Pat-
tern Recognition and Machine Learning (PRML) by Bishop Bishop [2006], which has 758
pages. The experiment compared two unorthodox paths:
Traditional Text RAG Pipeline:
1. Convert the PDF using a text extraction parser such as Docling1.
2. Get plain text out of it.
3. Chunkify it using Chonkie2.
4. Create embedding vectors using the Sentence Transformer3 library.
5. Put the vectors into a vector DB, Qdrant4.
Visual Embedding Pipeline:
1. Chomp and chunkify the PDF strictly into images—each page becomes a PNG
image (resulting in 758 PNGs).
2. Use a vision language understanding model, Qwen 35, to create image embeddings.
3. Put the image embeddings into the Qdrant vector DB.

5.3
Determining Which Approach Works Best
Which approach works best depends entirely on the kind of input data. If the input has
purely text, you can go for the text-based pipeline. If it has both text and images, you
will have to try different things to see what works.
In the real world, a watered-down version of the “No Free Lunch Theorem” applies:
a priori, you usually do not know which approach is best. You have to try all sorts
of algorithms. Our field is entirely based on tournaments. To build a serious, world-
class AI product instead of just applying cookie-cutter download libraries, you must run
tournaments to see which branch wins.
5.4

When building a RAG architecture, you can have a text-based approach and a visual
approach. If you can afford to maintain both indices, you should keep both of them as
search indices.

Basic Chunking Strategies
6.1
Fixed-Size Chunking
Fixed-size chunking treats an entire document string like a loaf of bread, going “chop
chop chop” to create fixed slices of text. While 512 tokens is a highly popular choice in
the industry, it is a bad practice. Choosing a size like 512 is too big and likely leads to
centroid delusion problems, blurring, and a complete loss of semantic purity.
Fixed-size string character chunking (e.g., cutting exactly every 1,000 characters) is a
disaster because it drops the knife and chunks right in the middle of words or sentences.
This completely destroys the semantic integrity of what was being said near the beginning
or the end of the chunk.
6.2
Sentence and Paragraph Boundary Chunking
A slightly smarter method is sentence boundary chunking, which cuts the text at the
end of a sentence. For example, in Mandarin, sentences are structurally structured to

Week 3 Summary
be semantically whole and form a complete unit, giving who, when, and where (e.g., “I
today in the restaurant ate dim sum”). Breaking at a sentence boundary here looks good.
However, sentence boundary chunking fails when facing the problem of endophora
across sentences, such as having a text say: “This is true. What is truth?” The context
of what “this” refers to is entirely lost.
To fix this, you can move to paragraph boundary chunking to break text at para-
graph limits. However, this approach is limited by varying author styles. For example,
the historic British author Sir Francis Bacon had a penchant for writing incredibly long
sentences, with one famous sentence starting at the top of a left-hand page and only
finishing at the bottom of the right-hand page. While American English styles eschew
long constructions for direct, short sentences, the legal community still uses highly com-
plex sentence and paragraph structures. Furthermore, journalistic styles like the New
York Times condense many different thoughts into single paragraphs because they know
readers have short attention spans.
Therefore, paragraph boundaries also fail because an argument may pursue into the
next paragraph, or the first paragraph may merely set up a premise that the second
paragraph establishes. It is never completely clear where a thought begins and ends.
6.3
Recursive Chunking
Recursive chunking is a graceful degradation approach. If you give the system an approx-
imate target size (like 100 or 512 tokens), the library will go to that size first and try to
shorten it gracefully. It checks if it can end up with one whole paragraph; if not, it down-
steps to see if it can at least end up with a complete sentence boundary. This recursive
approach is the default choice in many open-source libraries and is a good baseline way
to start.
6.4
Markdown/Structural Chunking
Markdown chunking breaks a document up structurally using headings, chapters, sec-
tions, and subsections. If a document has these structural elements available, you should
definitely exploit them. When an author consciously starts a new section, they are im-
plicitly stating: “Okay, now let’s talk of something else, we are done with this, let’s move
on to that.” Structural elements are a God-given blessing from the author telling you
exactly where the boundaries of a thought lie.
6.5
Hierarchical Chunking
Hierarchical chunking handles the dilemma where very short chunks have pure thoughts
but suffer from endophora, while large chunks have no endophora issues but suffer from
semantic impurity.

This strategy takes a hierarchical approach:
1. Start with a large parent chunk (e.g., 512 or 1,000 tokens) to capture a common
broad theme (similar to how an entire page in Bishop’s book is broadly about
probability).
2. Subdivide that parent chunk into smaller child chunks (e.g., 50 to 70 tokens).
While industry practice often advocates using overlapping smaller chunks to avoid
losing context, there is no strong or compelling evidence that text overlap makes a huge
difference with endophora.
When storing hierarchical chunks, you maintain a metadata table tracking the lineage:
which document corporate corpus a chunk belongs to, and exactly which parent chunk a
child belongs to. When a focused child chunk gets a nearest-neighbor hit during a query
search, you have two architectural choices for what to send to the LLM:
• You can pick up all the immediate neighbors of that child chunk and send them
together.
• You can completely bypass the small chunk and send the entire parent chunk (the
whole page or big piece of text) to the LLM.

You can implement this by providing a long paragraph of text to an LLM (like Chat-
GPT or Claude) with a prompt instructing it to break the text apart into semantically
coherent chunks of a rough target size.

7.2
Embedding-Based Semantic Chunking
This approach uses tiny encoder-style models to find semantic topic shifts geometrically.
You take the first sentence of a text, embed it, and project it as a vector onto the
hypersphere. You then embed the second sentence; if its vector lands close to the first,
you group them together. As you process the third sentence, you monitor the geometric
departure: how far is the new sentence vector from the previous sentence, or how far is
it from the running average centroid of the previous vectors? The moment the vector
distance departs past a set threshold, the system flags that a brand-new topic has started
and cuts a chunk boundary.
Because encoder-style models require only a single forward pass per piece of text, they
are incredibly tiny, cheap, and fast compared to LLMs. In the open-source community,

State-of-the-Art Chunking Methods
8.1
Contextual Chunking
8.1.1
The Paradox
Small chunks are ideal because they possess pure semantic thoughts, but they suffer from
a massive loss of context and break endophora references. The paradox headline is: How
do you keep smaller chunks without losing context? Summarizing paragraphs together
changes the text itself, and grouping chunks together simply builds a larger, semantically
impure chunk.
8.1.2
The Solution: Contextual Preface Enrichment
Figure 9: Illustration of Contextual Chunking.
The problem is that small chunks lose context.
The solution is using the whole
text, preface the chunk with 2-4 sentences if needed to restore context. The example
shows: original text “The capital of Germany is Berlin. Its population is 3.8 million
people.” The chunk is “It has 3.8M people.” The preface generated by LLM restores
context, and the augmented chunk becomes “The capital of Germany is Berlin. It has
3.8M people.” The key concept is Contextual Preface Enrichment - prepending brief,
model-generated explanations to tiny data segments clarifies ambiguous references while
maintaining semantic purity.

Developed as a powerful new genre of chunking by Anthropic11, the solution is blind-
ingly simple in hindsight. You generate small, focused chunks, but you do not store them
raw. Instead, you pass the chunk along with the entire surrounding document text to
an LLM. You instruct the machine to generate a brief preface of 2 to 4 sentences that
restores the missing background context, and then prepend that preface directly to the
chunk.
The critical implementation rule is that the LLM must never tamper with or rewrite
the original text of the chunk; it only generates the descriptive preface.
Example: If a raw text chunk splits mid-sentence and reads: “Its population is 3.8
million people.” (or 3.6 to 3.9 million based on recent data for Berlin), searching for
“Berlin” would completely miss this chunk because the word Berlin doesn’t exist in the
text. By passing it to an LLM with the wider text, the machine generates a preface: “The
capital of Germany is Berlin.” The stored chunk becomes: “The capital of Germany is
Berlin. Chunk: Its population is 3.8 million people.” Now, the pronoun ambiguity is
completely resolved.
8.1.3
Optimizing Compute: Determining What Context to Provide
From a pure semantic perspective, passing the entire document as context is ideal because
an explicit reference (like “Equation 3.6”) might be sitting four chapters prior. However,
sending a massive 50,000-token document to enrich a tiny 50-token chunk adds massive
compute costs, soaring token bills, and extreme latency for every single chunk call.
Reducing Context Scope
To optimize this process, you can limit the context win-
dow provided to the LLM to just the surrounding chapter, section, or a few proximal
paragraphs, assuming with high probability that the necessary context lives nearby.

Late Chunking
8.2.1
Core Insight
Late chunking14 Jin et al. [2024] represents the absolute state-of-the-art for text data.
Traditional RAG architectures chunk text early in human language space before creating
vectors, which permanently cuts off contextual links. Late chunking completely reverses
this sequence: you embed the entire document first, and defer chunking to the
machine space.
Instead of viewing chunking as physically cutting text, it treats chunking as merely
remembering a list of token index boundaries. If an entire document stream has M total
tokens (T1 all the way to TM), chunking is simply keeping a separate variable array of
index locations where the knife drops (e.g., boundary drops at token index 100, 187, and
251).

single final vector, vi, for each chunk.
8.2.2
Why It Works
Because the chunking occurs after the text has passed through the transformer’s self-
attention mechanism, every single token vector has already interacted with every other
token in the entire document.
Example: If a chunk contains the text “Its population is 3.8 million people,” the
token vector for “Its” has already looked across the document, identified “Berlin,” and
deeply soaked that contextual information into its mathematical representation. When
you mean pool that bucket, the resulting vector inherently “knows” it represents Berlin.
If a user queries for the population of Berlin, this chunk vector will easily bubble up as
the nearest neighbor in a K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN) vector search.

Shifting Paradigms
9.1
Visual Embeddings
9.1.1
The Concept
Instead of parsing a PDF into text chunks, you completely abandon text parsing. You
convert every page of a document into a standalone image (PNG) and pass those im-
ages directly into a Vision-Language Model (VLM) to generate page-level visual
embeddings.
When testing basic vision models like CLIP or SigLIP for this task, the retrieval
quality is incredibly poor—traditional text embeddings easily outperform them. How-
ever, when you use a highly advanced vision-language understanding model like Qwen
3 Vision15 model, the results become exceptionally sharp and precise.
9.1.2
When Visual Embedding Works Best
This alternative paradigm is heavily superior for complex scientific, technical, and en-
gineering documents (like Bishop’s PRML). It completely bypasses parsing data loss by
keeping the page layout completely whole. It naturally captures tables, distribution plots,
complex mathematical formulas, handwritten notes, and spatial headers that traditional
text parsers destroy.
9.1.3
The DeepSeek OCR Paper
A massive breakthrough occurred with a research paper called DeepSeek OCR DeepSeek-
AI [2025]. Despite the name, it is not an OCR tool at all. Instead of expanding the
context window of an LLM by feeding it raw text tokens, they did something completely
unorthodox: they treated massive walls of text as vision tokens.
They compressed vast streams of text into a highly dense visual representation, passed
it through an encoder with convolutional layers, and handed a dense latent visual em-
bedding to the LLM alongside the user’s query. Because images are incredibly dense

Key Takeaways
• The Chunking Dilemma: Text chunking forces a trade-off between semantic
purity (small chunks) and contextual integrity (large chunks), often causing issues
like the Centroid Delusion or broken endophora.
• One Size Does Not Fit All: Hardcoding chunk sizes (e.g., 512 tokens) is an archi-
tectural flaw; distinct document genres necessitate specialized chunking strategies.
• Contextual and Late Chunking: State-of-the-art methods like Contextual Pref-
ace Enrichment and Late Chunking preserve semantic meaning by retaining or
restoring surrounding context.
• Visual RAG for Complex Layouts: For technical documents, embedding entire
pages as images using Vision-Language Models (VLMs) captures spatial, tabular,
and graphical context that text parsers destroy.
• Maintain Multiple Indices: Running parallel search indices (e.g., text, visual,
dense, sparse) and merging results via re-ranking provides a massive retrieval per-
formance boost.
• Embedding Fragility: Embedding models remain highly sensitive to minor struc-
tural and grammatical changes; they must be deployed and evaluated with rigorous
caution.
