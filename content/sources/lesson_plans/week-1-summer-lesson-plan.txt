later engineering decision we make—how we cut documents, how
we retrieve, how we re-rank, how we ground, how we evaluate—is
downstream of the geometry of these spaces. Understand the geometry,
and the rest of the course becomes a series of sensible responses to its
quirks.

Search as Civilizational Infrastructure
Search is usually introduced as a solved problem. We type words;
a machine returns documents; we move on. Yet whole organisations
stumble not because they lack information but because they cannot
retrieve what they already possess. The contract that was signed, the
experiment that already failed, the customer who already complained—
all of it sits in some store, indexed by words that no longer match the
question being asked. Knowledge an organisation cannot find is, for
every practical purpose, knowledge it does not have.
Will Durant observed that civilisation is a stream with banks: the
stream carries the deeds we record, but the banks—the quiet, unremem-
bered work of keeping and retrieving knowledge—are what let each
generation begin where the last left off. Retrieval is bank-work. It is
unglamorous and it is load-bearing. A company’s collective memory is
only as useful as its retrievability, and the same is true of a language
model: a model that cannot reach the right fact at the right moment
will confidently invent one.
It helps to name the two ancient failure modes precisely, because the
whole course oscillates between them. Precision asks: of the things I
returned, how many were relevant? Recall asks: of the relevant things
that existed, how many did I return? A search that returns nothing has
perfect precision and useless recall; a search that returns everything
has perfect recall and useless precision. Every retrieval system is a
negotiated truce between these two, and much of the engineering
ahead—hybrid search, re-ranking, thresholds—is about moving the
truce to where your application needs it.
So we begin from a premise different from the usual one. Search is
not a technical convenience bolted onto a chatbot. It is the interface be-
tween human intent and collective memory, and Retrieval-Augmented
Generation is the architectural response to a hard limit of unaided
generation: a model’s parametric memory is frozen at training time,
finite in capacity, expensive to update, and unable to cite its sources.
RAG hands the model an open book at the moment of answering—and
the art of the course is making sure the book is open to the right page.

A Demonstration of Semantic Search
Let us watch the thing before we explain it. In the companion
notebook—the same one we used at the public talk—we pose queries
to a collection of images and passages and watch what comes back.
We ask, in words, about learning to love an animal, and the system

This should be unsettling if you still picture search as string-matching.
No substring of “to love an animal” occurs in the pixels of a photo-
graph. Something has been matched, but it is not text. What has been
compared is meaning, rendered as position in a space.
Here is the mechanism in one breath, to be unpacked all day. An
encoder turns the query into a vector eq; the same kind of encoder
has already turned every document into a vector ed; all of these live
in one shared space; and we retrieve the documents whose vectors are
closest to the query’s. When the query is text and the documents are
images, we use a model trained to place both modalities in one shared
space—the trick behind CLIP-style image–text retrieval— which is why
words can find pictures.
We will return, again and again, to three families of query on which
lexical and semantic search diverge. Abstraction: a query about “regula-
tory pressure” matching a passage that never uses those words but is
plainly about it. Emotion: matching a shared mood across very different
vocabulary. Intent: matching what the user is trying to accomplish
rather than what they literally typed. These divergences are not curiosi-
ties. They are the reason the rest of the course exists—and also, as we
will see in Act III, the seed of how semantic search goes wrong.

A distinction worth fixing now, because it will save you weeks of
confusion. The model that generates your answer (a decoder-style
LLM) is, in almost every production RAG system, not the same model
that embeds your documents for retrieval (an encoder-style embedding
model). The generator is eloquent and expensive and runs once per
answer; the embedder is silent and cheap and has already run over
every chunk in your corpus, placing each as a point in a space so that
similar texts land nearby. When we say “the embedding model” in
this course, we mean the encoder. When we say “the LLM” or “the
generator,” we mean the decoder. Conflating them is the single most
common Week-1 misconception, and it quietly corrupts one’s reasoning
about cost, latency, and failure modes for weeks.

Machines Think in “Machinese”
Here is the claim, stated plainly. Modern models do not manipulate
words, letters, or grammar rules. The first thing that happens to your
text is tokenisation—it is chopped into sub-word units, each mapped to
an integer, each integer mapped to a learned vector. From that point on,
every token, every passage, every image patch is a vector: an ordered list
of real numbers, a point in a high-dimensional space. Tensors—stacks
of such vectors—are the only objects the machine ever truly handles.
The native currency of machine cognition is not symbol and syntax; it
is proximity, similarity, and probability.

Polysemy: One Word, Many Points
Now a wrinkle that the early embeddings could not iron out.
Words have more than one meaning. A bank is a place for money and
also the edge of a river; a monarch is a sovereign and also a butterfly;
spring is a season, a coil, and a source of water.
This is polysemy,
and it is not a rare edge case—the most common words are the most
polysemous.
The first generation of embeddings (word2vec, GloVe) was static: one
vector per word type, computed once and frozen. Such a vector is forced
to be a compromise—the single point for “bank” is dragged toward
both “money” and “river” and ends up stranded between them, near
neither. For retrieval this is poison: a query about river ecology and a
query about interest rates would both match the same muddled “bank”
vector.
The fix is contextual embeddings. A transformer encoder (BERT and
its descendants) reads the whole sentence before placing any token, so
the vector for “bank” in “she sat on the river bank” lands far from the
vector for “bank” in “she deposited it at the bank”. Attention is what
makes this possible: each token’s representation is a weighted blend
of its neighbours, so context literally moves the point. Meaning is no

wizard but as a queryable, fallible store of geometric facts.

Embeddings as Points in High-Dimensional Space
Formally, an embedding model is a function f that maps a piece of
text x to a vector in Rd:

Typical d ranges from a few hundred to a few thousand. Each coor-

across all of them, holographically. What matters is the arrangement:
the relative positions of points, the neighbourhoods, the directions.
Retrieval, in this picture, is nearest-neighbour search. Given a query em-
bedding eq, we want the documents that maximise sim(eq, ed). When
the corpus is large, computing this against every document is too slow,
so we lean on approximate nearest neighbour (ANN) structures—inverted
file indexes, product quantisation, and small-world graphs such as
HNSW—a computational reality first made rigorous in the work of
Indyk and Motwani on similarity search in high dimension.
We will spend Week 2 onward worrying about what we embed (whole
documents? sentences? overlapping windows?) and how we index for

the geometry behaves the way our intuition expects. It does not.
The Curse of Dimensionality
Our intuitions about distance were trained in three dimensions, and

In high dimensions, randomness becomes oddly orderly. Draw

In low dimension these distances vary widely. In high dimension they
concentrate: almost all pairs end up at nearly the same distance from
one another, and the histogram of distances collapses toward a single
sharp spike.

that any useful structure must be put there by learning, and that the
margin between a true match and a random one can be thin—which
is exactly why re-ranking, hybrid search, and careful evaluation (later
instruments) earn their keep. The curse describes the space we are

margin learning carves out.

Anisotropy versus Isotropy: A Demonstration
Now a subtle and crucial distinction, and the one most often
muddled. Concentration of measure is a property of the space—it
would hold for points scattered uniformly at random. Anisotropy is
a property of the learned distribution of real embeddings within that
space, and the two are not the same. A distribution is isotropic if it
spreads roughly evenly in all directions; it is anisotropic if it crowds into
a narrow region—say, a thin cone—leaving most directions unused.
One clean way to measure it: take many random pairs of real embeddings
and average their cosine similarity. In a well-behaved (isotropic) space
this average should sit near zero, echoing the near-orthogonality of the
previous section. For raw, off-the-shelf contextual embeddings it does
not: the average cosine of unrelated texts comes out markedly positive,
because every vector points in roughly the same general direction.
Equivalently, the covariance of the embedding cloud is dominated by a
few large eigenvalues—a few directions soak up most of the variance.
This is the representation degeneration that afflicts naive embeddings, and
its consequence is brutal: when everything points the same way, “most
similar” loses its power to discriminate.
We make this visible.
We open Google’s Embedding Projector on a
small dataset with three subjects—physics, biology, and history. With
raw BERT embeddings, something disappointing happens: the three
subjects do not separate. Instead the whole cloud is squeezed into a
narrow, funnel-like cone, so even unrelated passages show high cosine
similarity. Switch to embeddings from a model trained specifically to
place semantically distinct texts apart, and the cloud opens up: physics,
biology, and history pull into three visibly separated regions, and finer
structure appears within each. The space has become more isotropic
and, crucially, more separated.
The lesson for a practitioner is immediate and sobering. An embed-
ding model is not a neutral ruler. Its geometry—how isotropic, how
separated—determines whether your retrieval can tell a physics passage
from a history one at all. Choosing, and later adapting, the embedder
is therefore among the highest-leverage decisions in the whole pipeline,
not a default to be accepted unexamined. Much of the engineering
ahead exists because real embedding spaces are messier than the tidy
isotropic ideal.

We can now assemble the simplest possible RAG system—and

the reasons trace directly back to Acts II and III.
Semantic near-misses. Because anisotropy compresses the space and
concentration thins the margin, the top-k “nearest” chunks may
be only marginally nearer than irrelevant ones. The system re-
trieves plausible-but-wrong neighbours and the generator, trusting
its context, states them confidently. This is the geometry of Sec-
tions and biting in production.
Polysemy collisions. A query about one sense of a word retrieves chunks
about another (Section )—the river bank when you meant the fi-
nancial one—unless context is preserved and representations are
good.
Lost in the cut. A fact split across a chunk boundary is unretrievable; no
embedding can recover information destroyed at ingestion. (Week 2:
chunking.)
Lexical blindness. A rare identifier—a part number, a statute, a gene—
may carry little semantic signal and be missed entirely by dense
search, even though a keyword index would have nailed it. (Hybrid
retrieval.)
No re-ranking. With matches concentrated near the noise floor, first-pass
similarity is too blunt to order the survivors well. (The re-ranking

Garbled questions. A misspelled, contextless, or multi-hop question em-

Ungrounded answers. Nothing checks that the generated answer is actu-

Today was deliberately expansive, and if some of it felt premature,
that is by design. We did not aim for mastery; we aimed for orientation.
We watched semantic search do the impossible, traced it to the discovery

Do not mistake breadth for vagueness. Every idea introduced today

instrument we build this summer is a response to a quirk of the space we met
today. Learn to see the space, and the rest of the course will feel less like a
syllabus and more like common sense.
