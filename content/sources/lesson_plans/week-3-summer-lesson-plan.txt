A Gentle Re-Entry
Twice now we have stood at the edge of a sphere. On Day 1
we argued that meaning has become geometry: a modern machine
does not reason in words but in vectors, points on the surface of a
high-dimensional globe, where nearness is kinship and direction is
meaning. We met the curse of dimensionality and its merciful twin,
the concentration of measure, and we watched a retriever fetch the
right passage about friendship with animals even though the winning
passage contained none of those words. On Day 2 — “The Shape
of a Decision” — we earned that geometry. We built, almost from
nothing, the softmax and the attention mechanism; we saw surprise
as the negative log-likelihood; and we ended on anisotropy, the quiet
tendency of embeddings to huddle in a narrow cone rather than spread
honestly across the hypersphere.
This week we take the next, deceptively simple step. Before a single
vector can be computed, a document must be turned into the things we
embed. That act has a name — chunking — and it is treated everywhere
as settled plumbing: parse the document, slice it into passages, embed,
retrieve. Our whole week is an argument that this moment deserves to
be slowed down, because it hides the first irreversible decision in the
entire pipeline.
Here is the single thread. A document is not one thought; it is an
ensemble of thoughts. To embed it well, we must cut it into units that
are each, as nearly as possible, a single coherent thought. But every cut
destroys something — a pronoun’s antecedent, a qualifier’s claim, a
figure’s caption. So the real question of the week is not “what is the
best chunk size?” It is two deeper questions, asked in order: should we
chunk at all? And if we must, how do we lose wisely?

The Central Provocation: To Chunk, or Not to
Chunk?
Nearly every tutorial on RAG begins the same way: fetch a doc-
ument, parse it, chunk it, embed the chunks, retrieve. The sequence
is so universal that questioning it feels almost heretical. Yet heresy,
grounded in evidence, is the birthplace of progress. Before we ask how
to chunk, we must ask whether chunking is the right abstraction at all.
Recent advances in vision-language models (VLMs) have unsettled
the orthodoxy. For whole classes of documents — the ones rich in
tables, charts, figures, and layout — embedding a page as an image can
outperform a carefully engineered parse-and-chunk pipeline. This is
not an anomaly; it is a signal. The correct stance, therefore, is not dogma
but empiricism: competing representations are placed in a tournament,
and the only arbiter is measurement.

The Six Mental Frames of the Week
Hold these six frames; everything today is a variation on them.
• Chunking is not preprocessing. It is an ontological decision about what
constitutes a thought.
• Every embedding is a lossy projection. The first projection into latent space
is irreversible.
• There is no universally optimal decomposition. Document structure, modal-
ity, and task jointly determine what works.
• Representation choice is experimental. Competing approaches must be run
as tournaments, not presumed.
• Small chunks trade purity for amnesia; large chunks trade context for
entanglement.
• Re-contextualization belongs downstream.
Context is best restored at
retrieval or generation time, not baked blindly into the embedding.
Act I: The First Cut Is the Deepest
Act I establishes the foundations: why the first projection is irre-
versible, why we chunk despite that, and why the innocent question
“how big should a chunk be?” has no universal answer.
The First Projection Is Irreversible
Before any mechanics, internalize a principle that governs everything
that follows: every embedding is a lossy projection, and the first projec-
tion from source into latent space cannot be undone. What the encoder
cannot see, the generator cannot recover.
Remember the children’s telephone game from Day 1 — the whisper
that degrades with each relay. Embedding is the first relay. If the
very first step introduces significant loss, no amount of downstream
sophistication — no reranker, no clever prompt — can restore it. This
is why chunking is not preprocessing. It is the first ontological decision
in your retrieval pipeline: what you decide to embed as one unit fixes
what the system can and cannot retrieve.

The Information Bottleneck
This loss is structural, not incidental.
The information bottleneck frames
representation learning as compression: given input X and target Y, find a
representation Z that keeps maximal information about Y while discarding the
rest of X,
LIB = I(X; Z) −β I(Z; Y),
where I(·; ·) is mutual information and β tunes the trade-off. When we embed
a chunk, we pass it through exactly such a bottleneck. If the chunk holds two
distinct thoughts — cows and the stock market — the bottleneck must crush both
into one point. The result is faithful to neither. This is why chunk composition
matters so profoundly.
Why We Chunk: Atoms of Thought
Suppose we have decided to parse and chunk. Why chunk at all
— why not embed whole documents? The answer flows from first
principles. A machine maps text to a point on a manifold, and that
mapping is meaningful only when each point represents one semantic
unit — one atom of thought. A document is, by definition, not a
single unit; it is a collection of them. Chapters are themes, sections are
topics, subsections are smaller units. The author’s act of dividing a
long document is itself explicit signal: here a context switch occurs; a new
thought begins.
The Mixing Principle
Write “the cow jumped over the moon.” One atomic thought. Now write
“Nvidia stock collapsed today.” A second thought, orthogonal to the first. In a
thousand-dimensional space these should land far apart. But concatenate them
into one embedding and you create a composite point representing a thought
that does not exist. In the concentration-of-measure framework from Day 1, that
composite lands in the equatorial band where noise lives — equidistant from both
clusters. Queried about cows, it is “kind of about cows”; queried about stocks,
it shows up too. It is noise on both. A single fixed-dimensional vector simply
cannot faithfully encode two orthogonal thoughts; the channel capacity is finite.
There is also a practical argument. Chunking enables selective retrieval.
A monolithic document vector forces the retriever into an all-or-nothing
verdict; it cannot say “Chapter 3 is relevant but Chapters 5 and 7 are
not.” Breaking the document into chunks lets the system surface exactly
the passages a query needs, with minimal surrounding noise.
And there is an economic argument. A long context incurs quadratic

The Boundary Problem and the 512-Token Default
The first pathology is deceptively simple: where do we draw the
line? A document is a continuous stream of meaning, and the question
of where one chunk ends has no ground truth. Too small, and we
lose context; too large, and we conflate distinct thoughts. There is
no universal right answer — optimal granularity depends on query
distribution, domain density, and downstream task.
The 512-Token Default
Fixed-size chunking is the worst offender, and 512 tokens is the most common
default. Let us reckon: a line holds 8–10 words, roughly 10–13 tokens; a page
holds 30–40 lines, hence ≈300–400 tokens. So a 512-token chunk is about
one page. What is the probability that an arbitrary page contains exactly one
coherent thought? For a dense newspaper article, very low. For a discursive
academic — a friend once left my lecture for coffee, returned an hour later, and
exclaimed, “You are still on the same point!” — also very low. The 512-token
default is not a considered choice; it is a baseline for expedience. Yet adopted
unreflectively it cascades through the entire pipeline, silently fixing what the
system can ever retrieve.
This is the first lesson of chunking: question the orthodoxy. Do not be
indoctrinated into the unthinking sequence every tutorial prescribes.
Think from first principles; understand what you gain and lose at each
step.

Act II is a field guide to failure. When a RAG system returns a
confidently wrong answer, the root cause is rarely a bad embedding
model or a poor prompt. More often it is a chunking decision made
months earlier, silently severing a connection the system can never
recover. Naming these pathologies gives you a diagnostic vocabulary —
so that when your system fails, you can ask precisely which kind of
failure it is.
Endophora and Broken References
Language refers to itself. “The cow jumped over the moon. It was
rather pleased with itself.” The pronoun “it” points backward to “cow”
— this is anaphora. Cut the two sentences into different chunks and the
second becomes incoherent. The mirror image is cataphora, a forward
reference: “He had no money. Yet John hoped to eat anyway.” Both
are forms of endophora — text referring to other text in the same
document — and both saturate technical and legal writing. “As we saw
in Equation 3.6. . . ” is meaningless if Equation 3.6 sits three chapters
away in another chunk.
This is the purity–amnesia trade-off in miniature: small chunks trade
purity for amnesia. A fifty-token chunk likely holds one thought, but
may lack the context to resolve its pronouns and abbreviations. In
Indian philosophy all meaning is said to live in one syllable — Om —
but that is meaning compressed by millennia of shared context, which
no embedding model possesses. In practice, meaning without context
is noise.
Discourse Structure and the Severing of Relations
Documents are not sequences of independent sentences. They
are hierarchical networks of rhetorical relations. Rhetorical Structure
Theory formalizes what you already feel: a document decomposes into
a tree of units bound by relations — cause–effect, contrast, elaboration,
evidence, concession. These are the logical skeleton of the argument.
Sever them and you do not merely lose information; you get structurally
misleading information that reads true but is not.
Consider:
“The drug reduced tumor volume by 40% (p < 0.01). However, the placebo group
showed a 15% reduction, suggesting a placebo effect. Adjusted for this baseline,
the drug’s net effect was a modest 25%, which did not reach clinical significance.”

Lawyers call this “taking out of context”; engineers call it a chunking
failure. They are the same thing. A clause reading “The licensee shall
have no obligation to pay royalties” is devastating — until you read the
preceding condition, “In the event that cumulative sales do not exceed
$1 million in any calendar year.” The conditional frame is the meaning.
The Negation Trap and Scope Failures
Scope failures deserve the most attention because they do not
merely lose information — they invert it. Negation is the most dan-
gerous culprit. “The meta-analysis found no significant evidence that
Compound X increases cardiac risk in patients under 65. However,
in patients 65–80 with hypertension, the data suggested a concerning
elevation.” Surface only the second sentence and the reassurance-with-
a-caveat becomes pure alarm; surface only the first and the caveat
vanishes. Neither is the truth. The truth is the relation between them
— and that relation lives exactly at the boundary where your chunker
cuts.
The same trap springs on conditional scope (“If the debt-to-income ratio
exceeds 43%, the following requirements apply. . . ” — chunk away
the condition and the requirements appear universal), on counterfactual
mood (“Had the rate stayed at 3%, the portfolio would have appreciated
12%” — the subjunctive that signals non-reality is the first casualty),
and on epistemic hedging.
Presupposition Accommodation
When a paper writes “we tentatively suggest, on preliminary evidence, that
mechanism Y may play a role,” the hedge scopes the whole claim. Three sen-
tences later the authors use the shorthand of an established fact, as authors do.
If the elaboration lands in a different chunk from the hedge, a tentative hypothe-
sis is silently promoted to certainty. Epistemologists call this presupposition
accommodation: encountering a claim without its epistemic frame, the reader
(or retriever) treats it as shared background. This is the most insidious chunking
failure — the one that produces no error signal at all. The system is confidently,
silently wrong.

Lexical Cohesion and Definition–Use Chains
Documents also cohere through implicit connections. Halliday
and Hasan identified five mechanisms of textual cohesion; the subtlest
is lexical cohesion — the cumulative effect of related words that build a
sustained semantic field, an isotopy. A legal brief that threads “negli-
gence,” “duty of care,” “breach,” “proximate cause,” and “damages”
across its pages makes each term resonate with the others. Chunk the
brief and each fragment carries only a few of these terms; the field
effect dissolves.
This matters geometrically. A chunk holding “breach” and “proximate
cause” embeds firmly in legal territory. A chunk holding “breach” alone
could mean a breached wall, a breach of protocol, or a whale breaching
the surface. The isotopy disambiguates; chunking destroys it; retrieval
suffers. This explains a genuinely puzzling production observation:
retrieval quality sometimes degrades as chunks get smaller, even though
smaller chunks should be more focused. The focused chunk has lost its
lexical company.
Bridging Inference and Genre Blindness
There is a connection subtler still, one with nothing explicit to
detect. “John walked into the room. The chandelier was magnificent.”
No pronoun, no connective — yet every reader knows the chandelier
is in the room. The definite article signals that the chandelier belongs
to the frame that “room” activated. This is bridging inference, and
Schank’s theory of scripts explains its reach: “the restaurant” activates
waiter, menu, bill, tip, so “she left a generous tip” is coherent only while
the restaurant script is live. Chunk it away and the embedding captures
the act of tipping but not its situation. In technical text the danger
sharpens: “the potential” (gravitational? electric?), “the spread” (bid–
ask? credit? yield?) — the surrounding text is the only disambiguator,
and chunking severs it invisibly.
A structural failure precedes all of these: genre blindness —
treating a legal contract, a novel, and a scientific paper as if they shared
one information structure. The contract is dense (every clause carries
force); the novel is sparse (pages of atmosphere around one pivotal
line); the paper has rigid implicit structure (abstract, methods, results,
each presupposing the others). Your chunker gives all three 512 tokens.

Pathologies in Embedding Space
The pathologies so far are textual. But chunking also distorts the
embedding space itself. The most important is the centroid delusion. A
chunk’s embedding is the centroid of its content — the average meaning.
Pack three distinct thoughts into a 400-token chunk and its embedding
sits equidistant from all three, which is to say near none of them. It is a
phantom: a location where no actual thought resides. You are hunting a
needle, and the embedding has helpfully averaged it with the haystack.
This is Simpson’s paradox in vector form: an aggregate can be far from
every constituent.
Anisotropy — our Day-2 antagonist — compounds this. In an anisotropic
space, large diverse chunks drift toward the mean direction of the cone
and become similar to everything yet particularly similar to nothing: the
“popular but useless” results that appear in every query’s top-k and
answer none of them. The cure is not filtering but re-chunking into
focused units whose embeddings escape the dense center.
When the Embedder Cannot See the Comma
Take Lynne Truss’s title Eats, Shoots & Leaves. With commas it is a gunslinger;
without, a panda. Now test three sentences — (1) “Eats, shoots, and leaves,”
(2) “Eats shoots and leaves,” (3) “A panda eats bamboo shoots and leaves.” Any
reader knows (2) is closer to (3) than to (1). In our classroom experiments,
many popular encoders get the ordering wrong: they weight lexical overlap
above the compositional semantics that punctuation controls. The embedder
cannot see the comma’s work. The lesson: if your chunks differ by syntactic
cues rather than vocabulary — as legal, medical, and regulatory text so often do
— your embedding model may simply fail to tell them apart. Embedding model
selection is itself a first-order chunking decision.
And yet, chunking lives on. After that catalogue of horrors, why
chunk at all? Because chunking is not born of ignorance; it is a com-
promise born of constraints. Context windows are finite: ten thousand

Act III: Two Roads — Better Chunking, or No
Chunking?
Act III offers two roads out of the swamp. The first improves the
cut: a spectrum of strategies that lose less. The second questions the
cut entirely: skip parsing and chunking, and retrieve the page as an
image. We walk both, then let measurement decide.
Road One, Part A: A Taxonomy of Chunking Strategies
Fixed-size chunking is the baker’s approach: treat text as a loaf and
cut equal slices. Simple, deterministic, fast — and wrong for text,
because a fixed boundary may bisect a thought, a sentence, even a word.
What is worse than a worm in your apple? Half a worm. Recursive
chunking improves matters by cutting preferentially at word, then
sentence, then paragraph boundaries — but it is still about size, not
meaning, and sentence boundaries barely exist in Chinese or Japanese
while paragraph boundaries swing wildly between a journalist and Sir
Francis Bacon.
Semantic chunking cuts where meaning actually shifts. Embed con-
secutive sentences, measure the distance between neighbors, and cut
where the distance spikes — a genuine topic transition. Libraries such
as Chonkie implement this. It is far better than fixed-size, at a modest
cost in compute. But it improves where we cut; it does not heal what
the cut severs. “It was rather pleased with itself” is correctly marked as
a unit’s end — and remains incomplete without its referent.

Hierarchical chunking embraces that documents have levels. Chunk
coarsely (sections, ≈300–500 tokens) and finely (atoms, ≈50–80 tokens),
building a tree of parents and children.
Parents preserve context;
children are coherent. At retrieval time the system chooses which to

Two Traps of Hierarchical Retrieval
Trap 1: the illusion of overlap. Overlapping windows ensure a severed sen-
tence survives intact in some neighbor, but do nothing for references spanning
paragraphs or pages, and they breed near-duplicate chunks that crowd the top-k.
Overlap is a local bandage on a global wound. Trap 2: the derailment of parents.
Substitute a parent for richer context and the LLM receives every sentence in
it — including the irrelevant ones — with no way to know which triggered
retrieval. A parent about supply-chain logistics that happens to mention pricing
will weave logistics into your pricing answer. The parent does not merely add
noise; it derails generation.

A Practical Pipeline
In practice the best systems combine these as layers of defense:
(1) markup chunking first — if the document has headings, sections, or
markdown structure, respect it; the author already marked the bound-
aries. (2) semantic hierarchical chunking with a tool like Chonkie,
coarse then fine, on meaning rather than size. (3) contextual chunk-
ing if budget allows, prepending inferred context to each fine chunk.
(4) late chunking before embedding, slicing contextualized latents at
the chosen boundaries. (5) store in a vector database with metadata —
section, position, parentage — intact.
This pipeline is a framework, not dogma. For any new domain, run
a tournament: try strategies, measure retrieval quality, and let the
empirical winner decide. No single technique solves all problems —
markup respects intent, semantic finds boundaries, contextual recovers
references, late chunking integrates global context. All chunking is wrong,
but some is useful: the goal is not to eliminate loss but to choose which
losses you can afford.
Road Two: Are We Barking Up the Wrong Tree?
Every strategy above shares a hidden premise: that we should parse
the document into text at all. Ask what parsing destroys. Picture a sin-
gle page of a physics textbook: running prose, the equation PV = nRT,
a pressure–temperature graph, a table of measurements, a diagram of
a piston with steam rising. A parser extracts “As shown in the figure
above, pressure and temperature are proportional. PV = nRT formal-
izes this.” What is lost? The spatial relationships, the diagram, the
graph, the tabular data — and the referential integrity, for “as shown
in the figure above” now floats untethered.

Exophoric Reference
Recall the dosa-shop directions from Day 1. The American directions — “Take
101, exit Page Mill, turn left” — are already a flat sequence; they survive parsing.
The Indian directions — “past the dosa shop, skip that one, the next dosa shop,
right at the cobbler, look for the peepal tree” — encode meaning through spatial
relationships between landmarks. Parsing flattens everything into the American
form, destroying exactly the exophoric texture that layout carries. A printed
page is a two-dimensional signal; parsed text is one-dimensional. Flattening 2D
to 1D is not a minor inconvenience — it is a fundamental reduction in channel
capacity.
Vision-language models invert the premise. In a striking result,
Alibaba Research took scientific documents and, instead of parsing,
rendered each page to an image, embedded the screenshots with a VLM,
and indexed those. Semantic search over page images dramatically out-
performed parse-and-chunk. The intuition echoes Day 1: an image is
high-bandwidth: a photograph of an elephant conveys texture, propor-
tion, and presence that no caption matches. VLMs often handle visual
information better than text because they preserve the spatial grammar
— table position-as-semantics, the numerator above the denominator,
indentation that carries legal force — that parsing flattens away.
How? The Vision Transformer cuts a page into a grid of 16 × 16-pixel
patches and treats each as a token; a 1024 × 1024 page becomes ≈4096
patch tokens, and self-attention discovers relationships between any
two regions — caption and figure, footnote marker and text. Crucially,
this is the same self-attention as a language model: to the transformer,
a patch and a subword are merely different tokens. That architectural
indifference to modality is the foundation of the whole enterprise.
ColPali made this practical at scale. It marries a vision encoder
to ColBERT-style late interaction: rather than crush a page into one
vector, it keeps a vector per patch and scores relevance by MaxSim
— for each query token, find its most similar page patch, then sum.
The system can, in effect, point at the region of the page that answers the
query, without ever extracting text. And a text query can match an
image patch because contrastive training (CLIP, SigLIP) projects both
modalities into one shared space: the bridge is geometric, not symbolic.

Where the Field Stands in 2026
ColPali was not the end but the beginning of a family.
The open ColVi-
sion line now spans ColQwen2.5, the smaller ColSmol, and the Qwen3-based
ColQwen3-4B, which posts state-of-the-art numbers on ViDoRe, the standard
visual-document-retrieval benchmark. The practical worry — multi-vector stor-
age — is being engineered away: HPC-ColPali compresses patch embeddings
via k-means quantization and dynamic pruning; training-free pooling toolkits
and pyramid indexing shrink the index further; and NanoVDR distills a 2 B
visual retriever into a 70 M text-only encoder — and ColModernVBERT, at
≈250 M parameters, comes within a fraction of a point of ColPali’s NDCG@5
with roughly 10× fewer parameters. The headline finding for your capstones:
financial PDFs — dense with tables and figures — show the widest gap, on
the order of ∼62% dense recall for text-only versus ∼84% for a ColQwen-class
visual retriever. If your corpus looks like that, page-image retrieval is no longer
exotic; it is the baseline you must beat.
Intellectual honesty demands the trade-offs. Vision retrieval gives
up exact text fidelity (a VLM’s reading is approximate — for character-
perfect keyword search, parsed text is indispensable); it gives up cross-
page attention (a 500-page PDF is 500 independently encoded images);
and it costs more compute per page (though the gap narrows each
hardware generation). It is precisely the first limitation — approximate
text fidelity — that makes the next result so surprising.
DeepSeek-OCR: Contexts Optical Compression
DeepSeek asked the inverse of the usual OCR question: how few vision tokens
does it take to faithfully encode a page of text? Their DeepEncoder — local-
attention (SAM), a 16× convolutional compressor, then global-attention (CLIP)
— produces as few as 64 vision tokens per page, from which a compact decoder
reconstructs the full text at 97% precision; even at 20× compression, accuracy
holds near 60%. The deeper message: if a decoder can rebuild 97% of a page
from 64 vision tokens, those tokens preserve nearly all its semantic content. So
we can take page images straight to vision tokens for retrieval, bypassing the
entire parse-chunk-tokenize- embed pipeline — every stage of which is a lossy
projection. The page image is already a remarkably efficient semantic container.

runs the experiment. If your documents are mostly prose — essays,

A Calibration Note
This session was dense. We argued that the first projection is irre-
versible; we built the case for chunking from atoms of thought, context
windows, precision, and cost; we toured a field guide of pathologies —
endophora, discourse relations, scope inversion, lexical cohesion, bridg-
ing inference, genre blindness, and the centroid delusion; we climbed
the taxonomy from fixed-size to semantic to hierarchical to contextual
to late chunking; and we put the whole orthodoxy on trial against
page-image retrieval and the 2026 ColPali family.
Hold the six frames. Chunking is not preprocessing but an ontological
decision; every embedding is a lossy projection; there is no universally
optimal decomposition; representation choice is experimental; small
chunks trade purity for amnesia; re-contextualization belongs down-
stream. Carry these and you will never again accept a 512-token default
without asking what it costs you.
Chunking is the first opportunity to lose information, and every step thereafter
struggles to recover what was lost at the beginning. The goal is not to lose
nothing — that is impossible. The goal is to lose wisely. And the wisest move of
all may be to transform the source before you ever cut it.
Next week we pull the thread we left dangling. Every technique today
assumed you start from the raw source and cut. But the source was
written for a human reader who fills gaps and reads between lines —
not for your retriever. What if, before chunking, you transformed the
source into something purer: reformulated, recontextualized, enriched
for retrieval? The source is the ore; the derivative artifact is the refined
metal — and it is the metal you should chunk. Picture abhi baaki hai,
mere dost — the movie is not over. This is the intermission.
