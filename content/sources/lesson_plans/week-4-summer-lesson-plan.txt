A Gentle Re-Entry
Last week we measured the size of thoughts. We chunked. We
argued about where a thought begins and ends, catalogued the patholo-
gies of cutting text into pieces, and weighed the remedies: semantic
boundaries, hierarchical chunks, contextual retrieval, late chunking. We
even asked the heretical question — whether to chunk at all, given that
a vision-language model can embed the page as an image and skip
parsing entirely.
But notice what every one of those techniques quietly assumed.
Each was still indexing the original text. Semantic chunking cut the
original differently; contextual retrieval decorated the original with a
sentence of context; late chunking pooled the original in latent space.
The substrate never changed. We were always searching the words the
author wrote.
This week we abandon that assumption, and the whole posture of the
course shifts with it. We stop asking “how do I cut the document well?”

What if the best thing to search is text the document never literally said?
Here is the single thread to hold. A document is written for a
reader — to be argued, defended, taught, or filed away. It is almost
never written to be found by a short, blunt query typed into a search
box. So before we index a document, we will manufacture new textual
objects from it — derivative artifacts — each one written, this time,
for the searcher. We will search those. And then, having found the
right place, we will show the reader the original. That last sentence is
the entire week compressed: we search derived artifacts; we show original
results.

Act I: The Purpose Problem
Act I makes one claim and makes it stick: text resists search not by
accident but by design, because every text was written for someone,
and that someone was never the search engine. We will feel this before
we fix it — first through a story, then through the genres you read every
day.
The Exam That Taught Me About Indexing
Let me tell you about two students preparing for the IIT en-
trance examination — the gateway exam that, in India, decides
futures. Call them Meera and Ravi. Meera reads physics to understand
it. She lingers on Newton’s third law until she can feel the symmetry
of forces in her bones; she derives, she doubts, she rebuilds. Ravi does
something different. Ravi has a coach, and the coach has a stack of
ten thousand past questions. Ravi drills until, the instant he sees “two
blocks, one pulley, coefficient of friction µ,” his hand is already moving.
He is not understanding the problem. He is matching it.
Who is right? It is a trick question — they have different purposes, and
purpose dictates representation. Meera is building a model of the world.
Ravi is building an index from question-shapes to answer-procedures. If
you tested them on deep conceptual transfer, Meera wins. If you tested
them on “answer two hundred questions in three hours,” Ravi wins,
and it is not close.

Every Text Wears a Costume
Text is never neutral. It is dressed for an audience and a purpose, and
the costume changes everything about how it can be found. Consider
the same underlying finding — a drug reduced thirty-day mortality by
four percent — as it would be written in six different rooms:
The paper hedges. In IMRaD form — Introduction, Methods, Results,
and Discussion — the number lives in Results, its meaning lives
in Discussion, and the Abstract softens both with “may suggest a
modest benefit.” The paper is written for a skeptical peer reviewer,
so it distributes its claim across structure and qualifies it into safety.
The legal brief forecloses. It is written for an adversary, so it anticipates
objections and buries the operative clause in “notwithstanding the
foregoing.”
The earnings call consensus-softens. It is written for shareholders and
lawyers at once, so responsibility is distributed and every claim
wears a helmet.
The press release sells. The textbook teaches. The tweet provokes.
Six costumes, one truth, and here is the thing that should unsettle you:
a user’s query — “does the drug reduce deaths?” — matches none of
them well. The query is naked. The text is in costume. Retrieval is
the awkward party where the naked query must recognize a friend
through six disguises.

Act II: The Derivative Artifacts — and the Proof
Act II is where we build the toolkit and then put it on trial. We
meet four derivative artifacts — factoids, rewrites, QA pairs, and the
pattern that binds them — but we do not take their superiority on faith.
Between meeting factoids and meeting rewrites, we run a bake-off on a

Factoids: Distilling the Atoms of Meaning
A factoid is the smallest textual unit that still means something.
The chemistry analogy is more than decorative: a molecule is the
smallest unit that retains the properties of a substance. Split water and
you get hydrogen and oxygen — useful, but no longer wet. A factoid
is the semantic molecule: split it and you have fragments that are no
longer about anything.
Consider a paragraph from a geography text:
Berlin, situated on the banks of the River Spree, serves as the capital of Germany
and is its most populous city, with a metropolitan population exceeding six mil-
lion residents. The city has been a center of European politics since reunification
in 1990.
This single paragraph hides at least five factoids: Berlin sits on the Spree;
Berlin is the capital of Germany; Berlin is Germany’s most populous
city; Berlin’s metropolitan population exceeds six million; Berlin has
been a political center since 1990. Each is a complete, standalone claim
— and not one of them appears in the paragraph as an isolated sentence.
The phrase “the capital of Germany” is welded into a longer clause.
Extraction is the act of prying it loose and making it self-contained,
resolving the pronouns and the implicit references so that the factoid
can stand alone in the index with no memory of its paragraph.
The craft lives in that self-containment. A lazy extractor returns
“It is the capital” — useless, because “it” has been severed from “Berlin.”
A good extractor returns “Berlin is the capital of Germany.” This is
why factoid extraction is the part of the pipeline that rewards prompt
optimization most: the prompts we use in production are the product
of heavy DSPy-driven tuning over our own corpora, precisely because
the difference between a self-contained factoid and a dangling fragment
is the difference between a retrievable thought and noise.

A passage rewrite reformulates a span in plain, direct, search-friendly

rewrite is a shadow document, indexed alongside it, invisible to the user
but highly visible to the retriever.
Take the clause:

A search-friendly rewrite:
The licensor can terminate the agreement with 30 days’ written notice,
regardless of Section 12(b).
The rewrite embeds closer to how a user would ask — “can the licensor
end the agreement early?” — yet the original clause, with all its opera-
tive force, remains the authoritative passage handed to the generation
model. This is the understudy who auditions for the retriever so that
the star can take the stage to perform.
QA Pairs: Planting Query-Shaped Objects
Of all the artifacts, the question-answer pair is the most mis-
chievous — and the most direct attack on the costume problem.
The idea is simple: given a chunk, have an LLM generate the plausible
questions that chunk could answer, paired with their answers, and
embed both.
Why does it work?
Because retrieval is a matching problem, and
matching is easiest when both sides speak the same dialect. A dense
retriever compares a query embedding with document embeddings.
Ordinarily this is a cross-genre comparison — a question measured
against declarative prose. But if the indexed object was itself derived
from a question, the comparison becomes same-genre: question against
question. You have planted a query-shaped decoy that the retriever
should find.
A good QA pipeline generates three to eight questions per chunk,
varied in specificity (from “what does this section discuss?” to “what
learning rate did experiment 3 use?”), in formulation (keyword-ish to
natural-language), and in abstraction (factual to interpretive). Each pair
stores a pointer back to its source chunk — because at generation time
the model must see the chunk, not the synthetic question. And note the
failure mode you will inspect in lab: a careless synthetic question can
mislead the retriever, promising an answer the source does not actually
contain. Generation hygiene matters.

Act III: Altitude — Summaries, RAPTOR, and
the Corpus
Everything in Act II was local. Factoids, rewrites, and QA pairs
operate at the altitude of a single passage. But some questions cannot be
answered from any one passage, however well we extract it — because
their answer is not in the document; it is about the document. For those
we must gain altitude, and then, at the very end, we must leave the
single document behind entirely.
Abstractive Summarization: Trading Detail for Altitude
A summary is not a compression. It is a change of altitude. The
word “compression” suggests the same content packed tighter into
a smaller box. Summarization is not that. It is ascent in a balloon:
you rise, and large structures invisible from the ground swim into
view — the shape of the coastline, the pattern of the fields — while
the fine details that filled your vision on the ground vanish. You trade
resolution for scope. Neither view is truer. They are different altitudes
over the same land.
This is why summaries are the complement of factoids, never their re-
placement. Factoids sacrifice context for precision; summaries sacrifice
precision for context. Some queries are unanswerable from the ground:

Summaries at Many Granularities
Once you accept that altitude is a dial, the natural question is:
why choose one setting? A per-section summary answers “what does
the methods section cover?”; a per-document summary answers “what
is this paper’s contribution?”; a per-corpus summary answers “what
is this whole collection about?” Each is cheap to generate relative to
its value, and each catches a band of thematic queries the others miss.
The instinct of the week — one source, many representations — applies
along the altitude axis just as it did along the precision axis. And once
you have summaries at several fixed altitudes, you are one small step
from making altitude continuous. That step is RAPTOR.
RAPTOR: The Zoom Lens
RAPTOR takes summarization and gives it recursive structure.
The algorithm is elegant enough to state in five lines:
1. Start with leaf-level chunks.
2. Cluster nearby chunks by embedding similarity.
3. Summarize each cluster into a new node.
4. Repeat: cluster the summaries, summarize the clusters.
5. Stop at a single root (or a few high-level nodes).
The result is a tree. The leaves are fine-grained chunks; the middle
nodes are progressively more abstract summaries; the root is a synopsis

From One Book to a Library: RAPTOR’s Definitive Articles
Here is the idea I most want you to carry out of the room today.
Run RAPTOR over a single document and its upper nodes are sum-
maries of that document. But run it over a corpus — a hundred papers,
a thousand support tickets, every internal memo on a topic — and the
clusters no longer respect document boundaries. A cluster gathers the
chunks that are about the same thing, drawn from wherever they live. Its
summary is therefore something new: not a digest of any one source,
but a synthesis across all of them — a definitive article on a topic that no
single document contains.
Imagine asking a shelf — PRML and Hastie–Tibshirani–Friedman’s
Elements of Statistical Learning together — “what is overfitting?” A naive
system returns Bishop’s best chunk or ESL’s best chunk. A corpus-
level RAPTOR node returns one coherent answer that has already
reconciled both treatments into a single, canonical paragraph. That is
the definitive-article property, and it is the reason this technique is not
merely “summarization with extra steps.”
And this is exactly where we stop — on purpose. To build that
corpus-level article well, you eventually want to cluster not by raw
embedding proximity but by relational structure: which entities and
ideas are genuinely connected across documents. That is community
detection over a graph, and it is a different and beautiful machinery
— network analysis, modularity, the Leiden algorithm, and the move
“from local to global” — that deserves its own day. Notice, though,
that you have already met its seed twice today: once in RAPTOR’s
soft clustering of a corpus, and once in the simple observation that a

The bake-off from § becomes a measured experiment. Ingest the
first three chapters of PRML three ways: (a) a raw-chunk index; (b) a
factoid index; (c) a QA-pair index. Keep all three live — remember, we
search the raw chunks too — and fuse their results.
Assemble a query set of twenty questions in three bands: pointed
factual (“what degree polynomial does Bishop fit in Figure 1.4?”),
vocabulary-mismatch (“why do the weights blow up when the curve
gets too wiggly?”), and synthesis (“how do model complexity and
dataset size jointly drive overfitting?”). For each arm, record Recall@k,
MRR, and — the metric that matters most — a human answer-ability
judgement: could you write a correct, self-contained answer from what
came back? Tabulate. The expected shape: raw chunks hold their own
on the pointed band, derivatives pull ahead on the mismatch band, and
the synthesis band is where everyone struggles and RAPTOR (Lab 3)
earns its keep.

Lab 3: A RAPTOR Tree at Two Altitudes
Implement the cluster-summarize-recurse loop for two or three

Lab 4: The Multi-Representation Tournament
Finally, assemble every index and ask which ones are paying rent.
Combine raw chunks, factoids, QA pairs, and RAPTOR nodes behind
one fused retriever. Then ablate: remove each representation in turn
and measure the system-level drop. The deliverable is an honest table
— one row per representation, one column per query band — showing
exactly where each artifact earns its storage and its ingestion cost. Some
representation will surprise you by contributing almost nothing on your
corpus; that is a finding, not a failure, and it is the kind of finding that
separates an engineer from a cargo-culter.

Essential Readings
These are required. Each develops a thread we opened today.
• Chen et al., Dense X Retrieval: What Retrieval Granularity Should
We Use?
The definitive study on proposition-level retrieval and the source of
the factoid idea. Establishes that retrieval granularity is not a fixed

to understand why an atom beats a paragraph for pointed queries.

A Calibration Note
This session was dense, and it turned on a single inversion. For
three weeks we treated the document as the thing to be indexed. Today
we treated it as raw material — a substrate from which to manufacture
better things to index. We met four local artifacts (factoids, rewrites, QA
pairs, and the pattern that keeps them safe), and two altitudes (sum-
maries, and RAPTOR’s continuous zoom), and we ended by leaving the
single document behind for the corpus-level article that no document
contains.
The stories were not decoration. Meera and Ravi were the whole ar-
gument: representation follows purpose, and the searcher’s purpose
is not the reader’s. The six costumes were the same argument in a
different room. And the Centroid Tug-of-War was not a game bolted
onto the mathematics — it was the mathematics, standing up and walk-
ing around. The equation cos(q, ek) ≥cos(q, epara) and the student
walking from the marooned centroid to the “six million” vertex are the
same sentence, told twice.
Text is written for a reader; search needs an artifact written for the query. So
we manufacture the artifact, we search it — alongside the raw chunks, never in
their place — and we show the reader the original. We retrieve the derivative.
We generate from the source.
Next week we climb from the book to the library. The soft clustering
hiding inside RAPTOR, and the corpus-level article we glimpsed at the
end, return as network analysis and community detection. We will meet
GraphRAG — and its memory-augmented cousin, Memory-GraphRAG
— and discover that “find the communities, then summarize each one”
is the same instinct as today’s, scaled from one document’s paragraphs
to a whole corpus’s ideas. Bring the prism. We are about to hand it a
much larger light.
