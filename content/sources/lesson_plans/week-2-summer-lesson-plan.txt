Softmax, Built by Hand
Let us start with a fistful of numbers, and a piece of graph paper.
Suppose a small vision model has looked at a photograph and emitted
three raw scores — call them logits — for the three things it has been
taught to recognise: a cow, a duck, a sofa.
Say the numbers are
2.0, 1.0, and 0.1. Draw them as three bars. The cow bar is tallest,
the duck shorter, the sofa barely off the ground. These numbers are
honest reports of the model’s enthusiasm, but they are useless as a
decision. They are not probabilities: they do not sum to anything in
particular, one of them could have been negative, and there is no sense
in which the cow bar is “2.0 units of belief”. We want to turn this raw
enthusiasm into something we could bet on — a distribution that sums
to one, where each height is the machine’s honest probability that the
photograph is a cow, a duck, or a sofa.
The first move is to exponentiate. Replace each logit zi by ezi. Three
things happen at once, and all three are the point. First, every exponen-
tial is positive, so a negative logit can no longer embarrass us — e−3
is small but perfectly legal. Second, the exponential is monotonic, so
it never reorders the bars: the cow was tallest and stays tallest. Third
— and this is the one to feel in your bones — the exponential amplifies
gaps. A logit lead of 1.0 becomes a multiplicative lead of e ≈2.718. The
rich get richer. Re-draw your bars after exponentiating: e2.0 ≈7.39,
e1.0 ≈2.72, e0.1 ≈1.11. The cow now towers.
Now the second move: normalise. Add the three exponentials together —
here, 7.39 + 2.72 + 1.11 = 11.22 — and divide each by the total. The bars
become 0.659, 0.242, 0.099. They sum to one. We have a distribution.

Surprise, and Why It Must Start at Zero
We can now make beliefs. But a belief is worthless until the world
tests it, and the test has a feeling attached: surprise. Surprise is the raw
sensation out of which we will build the entire machinery of learning,
so let us get its shape exactly right, and let us do it with weather.
Suppose this morning you believed, with 99% confidence, that it would
rain — and it rained. How surprised are you? Barely at all. You
knew. Now suppose instead you had judged rain a 1% long shot —
and it poured. Now you are astonished. The lesson is that surprise is
a decreasing function of the probability you assigned to the thing that
actually happened. The more confidently you expected it, the less it
startles you when it arrives; the more you dismissed it, the harder it
hits.
What is the simplest function that decreases as p grows and blows up
as p →0? A student’s first, honest guess is the inverse, 1/p. And it is
a good guess! As p shrinks toward zero, 1/p shoots to infinity — the
1% rain that ruins the picnic gives a surprise of 100, which feels right.
But the inverse has one small, fatal flaw, and finding it is the whole
game. Consider absolute certainty: p = 1, the event you were sure of,
and it happens. Your surprise should be exactly nothing — zero. But
1/p = 1/1 = 1. The inverse insists you remain one unit surprised by
the utterly inevitable. Its range is [1, ∞) when we need [0, ∞). The floor
is in the wrong place.
So we ask a sharper question: how do we keep the lovely “blows up
near zero” behaviour of 1/p, while pulling the floor down to zero at
p = 1? The easiest repair in all of mathematics: take the logarithm.

The Gradient the Softmax Allows
One technical grace note that explains why this particular pair —
softmax in front, negative log-likelihood behind — became the universal
coupling, used in virtually every classifier on earth. It is not only that
each piece is sensible. It is that when you compose them and ask for
the gradient — the thing gradient descent actually needs — an almost
miraculous simplification occurs. Let the true answer be a one-hot
vector y (a one on the correct class, zeros elsewhere), let p = softmax(z)
be the predicted distribution, and let the loss be the cross-entropy
−∑i yi log pi. The gradient of that loss with respect to the logits is
simply

∂z

no awkward factors and no vanishing. If the model put 0.7 on the right
class, the gradient there is 0.7 −1 = −0.3, a gentle pull upward; if it
put 0.9 on a wrong class, the gradient there is 0.9 −0 = 0.9, a firm
shove down. The exponential of the softmax and the logarithm of the
loss were, in a sense, built for each other — the log undoes the exp at
exactly the right moment — and their child is a gradient of disarming
simplicity. This is the engineering reason the cat and its judge are
always seen holding hands: together they are not just correct but kind
to the optimiser.

Is a 0.7 Really a 70%? A Word on Calibration
A caution, before we trust these numbers too far. The softmax
always hands back something that looks like a probability — it sums
to one, each entry sits in [0, 1] — but looking like a probability and
being one are different things. A model is calibrated if, among all the
times it says “0.7,” the answer really is correct about 70% of the time.
Modern deep networks are notoriously over-confident: trained hard
to minimise negative log-likelihood, they learn to push their winning
logit very high, so a reported 0.99 may correspond to a true accuracy
of only 0.90. The distribution is sharp where it has no right to be.
Why should you, building retrieval systems, care? Because the moment

Before the Vector: Breaking Text into Tokens
We have been speaking of “a word becoming a vector” as if a word
were the natural atom of text. It is not, and the gap is worth one careful
subsection, because it explains a great many things you will otherwise
find baffling — why models stumble on rare names, why they count
letters poorly, why a typo like runnign is not as alien to them as it looks.
Before any vector exists, raw text must be chopped into units the model
knows, and those units are tokens, not words.
A modern tokenizer learns its vocabulary from data by a procedure
like byte-pair encoding: start from individual characters and repeatedly
merge the most frequent adjacent pair into a new unit, until you have
a vocabulary of some tens of thousands of subword pieces. Common
words survive as single tokens (the, river); rare or complex ones shatter
into familiar fragments (tokenization might become token + ization; an
unusual surname might dissolve into a handful of syllables). Each token
in the vocabulary owns a row in a giant lookup table — the embedding
matrix — and that row is its initial vector, the raw, context-free location
it occupies before attention has said a word.
So the true pipeline, stated once and exactly: text is split into subword
tokens; each token is looked up to its row in the embedding matrix,
giving a context-free vector; positional information is added so order
is not lost; and then the stack of attention layers we are about to build
turns those raw vectors into context-aware ones. When we say “the
word bank becomes a vector,” we are compressing these first steps for
the sake of the story. Keep the full chain in mind, though — it is the
reason the geometry has anything to work on.

Why Machines Must Speak Machinese
Last week we asserted that machines reason in vectors — “machinese”
— and that text, to a machine, is a set of locations on the surface of a
high-dimensional globe. We took it on faith. Today we earn it, because
there is a genuine reason, and the reason is gradient descent itself.
Suppose, early in training, a model holds a word x and believes it sits
closest in meaning to a word y. Suppose we know better — the truth is
that x belongs near z. Learning must move x away from y and toward z.
But here is the difficulty: gradient descent only ever makes infinitesimal
moves. It does not leap; it nudges, by a tiny step proportional to the
gradient, ten thousand times over. So we need a space in which x can
be nudged a little — a space where there exist positions between the
meaning of one word and the meaning of another.
Now ask: can we make such a nudge in the discrete world of the lexicon?
Between bat and ball, is there a word in the dictionary meaning “99%
bat, 1% ball”? There is not. The lexicon is a scatter of isolated islands
with nothing but void between them; you cannot stand a hair’s breadth
off the island of bat. A discrete vocabulary offers gradient descent no
place to put its small steps, and so it offers no gradient at all. But the
surface of a globe — a smooth, continuous manifold — offers infinitely
many positions between any two points. On a manifold you can slide
x a whisker away from y and toward z, and tomorrow a whisker
more. That is why meaning must become geometry: because learning
is gradient descent, gradient descent needs small steps, and only a
continuous manifold has room for them. The discrete word was always
a prison; the vector is the parole.
So the machine, denied the dictionary of discrete words, builds itself a
geometric dictionary instead: every word becomes a vector — a location
— and so does every query. The fuzzy lookup we did by hand a mo-
ment ago is now a computation the machine can actually perform and,
crucially, differentiate: proximity becomes a dot product, the weighting
becomes a softmax, and the whole librarian’s act becomes a smooth
function the optimiser can push downhill. Machinese is not an affec-
tation. It is the only language in which learning by gradients can be
spoken.

The Missing Rung: from Words to a Single Vector
There is a rung in this ladder that is easy to skip and fatal to omit,
so let us set it firmly in place. Self-attention gives us a contextual
vector for every word in a chunk — nine words in, nine context-aware
vectors out. But when we go to build a retrieval system, what do we
put into Qdrant? Not nine vectors per chunk — one. A search index
wants a single point per document so that a single query point can
find it. How do we collapse nine contextual word-meanings into one
chunk-meaning?
The operation is called pooling, and the workhorse is the gentlest thing
imaginable: mean-pooling, the simple average of the token vectors. (An
older recipe took only the vector of a special [CLS] token; mean-pooling
reliably beats it for retrieval, because averaging listens to every word
rather than betting everything on one.) The average of the nine context-
aware vectors is the chunk’s embedding — the single location on the
globe that we will index, and against which incoming queries will be
compared. So the full pipeline now stands complete in your mind:
tokens become vectors, attention makes those vectors context-aware,
pooling fuses them into one chunk vector, and that is what lives in the
database. Hold this rung. In Week 3, when we agonise over where to
cut a document into chunks, the whole anxiety will be about what this
average ends up meaning.

Anisotropy: When Attraction Is Not Enough
Last week we glimpsed a flaw in raw embeddings and named its
cure in passing. Now we face it squarely. When a model is trained
only to predict words — only the masked-word game, only negative
log-likelihood — it learns a representation that is powerful but geomet-
rically lopsided. All the vectors drift into a narrow cone, crammed into
one corner of the globe, so that even two unrelated sentences look fairly
similar because everything looks fairly similar. This pathology is called
anisotropy — the space is not the same in all directions; it has a strong
grain — and it is poison for retrieval, because retrieval lives or dies on
the gaps between things.
What is missing is a repulsive force. And this is precisely what con-
trastive learning supplies. The recipe is almost embarrassingly intuitive:
show the model pairs of things that should be close (a sentence and
its paraphrase; an image and its caption) and pull them together, but
also show it pairs that should be far (a sentence and an unrelated one)
and push them apart. Attraction and repulsion, together, do what
gravity alone never could: they spread the representations out to fill
the sphere, restoring the isotropy — sameness-in-all-directions — that
makes distances meaningful again. This is the family of methods —
Sentence-BERT, SimCSE, and their kin — that turns a raw language
model into a genuine embedding model fit for search.

Indexing the Globe: Finding a Neighbour Without Asking Every-
one
There is a hard practical problem lurking under “find the nearest
point on the globe,” and it is worth seeing clearly, because it is what a
vector database spends its life solving. If you have ten million document
vectors and a query arrives, the honest way to find the closest is to
compute ten million dot products and take the best — an exact search
that is correct and far too slow for an interactive system. We need to
find the nearest neighbour without measuring our distance to everyone,
and the willingness to be occasionally, slightly wrong about it is what
buys the speed. This is approximate nearest-neighbour search, and it is
the engine inside Qdrant.
The dominant idea is beautifully geometric: build a navigable small-
world graph (the Hnsw algorithm) over the points, connecting each
vector to a handful of near neighbours and a few long-range “shortcut”
links. To answer a query you do not scan the globe — you walk it:
start anywhere, hop to whichever neighbour is closer to the query,
and repeat, descending greedily toward the query’s region like water
finding a drain. The long-range shortcuts let you cross the globe in a
few hops (the “small-world” property, the same six-degrees structure
that connects distant strangers in a social network), and the local links
let you home in precisely once you are close. A few dozen hops, not ten
million comparisons. Once again the geometry is not decoration: the
search is literally navigation across the surface we have been describing
all day, and its speed comes from the shape of the space.

Rabbit Hole 1: Softmax Is Maxwell–Boltzmann
Why does the softmax keep appearing? Here is the first deep answer.

your belief as widely as the constraints permit, commit to no order you

Rabbit Hole 3: Attention Is Associative Memory
One last descent, the deepest. We have called attention a fuzzy
dictionary lookup. There is a formal name for a system that stores
patterns and retrieves the whole from a part: an associative memory, and
its classical model is the Hopfield network. Give an old Hopfield network
a noisy or partial pattern and it relaxes, step by step, into the nearest
stored memory. Its flaw was capacity: it could reliably store only a
handful of patterns before they blurred into one another.
In 2020, Ramsauer and colleagues proved something startling. Store a
set of patterns as the columns of a matrix K, and define an energy land-
scape over the query state q whose wells sit at the stored patterns. The
classical Hopfield network used a quadratic energy, and quadratic wells
are shallow and few — hence the tiny capacity. The modern network
uses an energy built from a log-sum-exp of the query’s alignments with
the stored patterns,

A Handful of Confusions Worth Pre-empting
When you return to this document in a later week, short on time
and trying to remember what today actually established, these are the
slippery points most worth having straight. Each is a place a careful
person stumbles, and each has a one-line fix.
The softmax is not a maximum. It is a soft argmax — a smooth, differen-
tiable pointing-at the largest entry, not the largest entry itself. Its
whole reason for existing is that the true max has no useful gradient.
Logits are not probabilities, and softmax outputs are only calibrated probabilities if you checke
A tidy 0.9 is a decision; whether it means “90% of the time” is a
separate, empirical question, and modern models are usually over-
confident until cooled.
Attention is not “similarity”. If it were, equal edit distance would force
equal weights — recall teh. Attention scores are learned alignments
through the WQ, WK projections, so they encode priors a fixed dis-
tance metric never could.
Cosine equals dot product only on the unit sphere. Forget to normalise
and the dot product silently rewards long vectors; a few high-norm
chunks then dominate every result. Normalise, and the three notions
of nearness coincide.
Negative log-likelihood scores only the truth. It grades the probability you
placed on the outcome that actually happened; the rest of your dis-
tribution is free. This is why confident-and-wrong is punished so
savagely — the one term that mattered was tiny.
Perplexity is not the loss; it is two-to-the-loss. The loss is average surprise
in bits; the perplexity, 2loss, is the effective number of choices. A
loss of 3 bits and a perplexity of 8 are the same statement.

If it feels like a great deal, notice that it was, in the end, one thing.
We did not learn a dozen mechanisms today; we learned a single
mechanism in a dozen disguises, and we learned to recognise it by
its grin. The morning’s stories — the rainy afternoon, the cow and
the duck, Emily at the back of the room — were not scaffolding to be
kicked away once the equations arrived. The equations are the stories,
made exact. A model that learns is a model becoming less surprised; an
embedding is a fossil of that unsurprise; attention is a librarian; and the
whole of it is the Cheshire Cat, turning preferences into probabilities
while assuming as little as it possibly can.
Let me leave you with a thought larger than any equation. What
we built today is the machinery by which a machine decides what
a word means, what a document is about, which photograph belongs
beside which paragraph. These are not small powers. When we hand a
system the authority to retrieve our laws, our medicine, our histories,
and to decide which fragment of the human record surfaces in answer
to a question, we are handing it a share of the custody of meaning
itself. A softmax that quietly over-commits, an embedding space that
quietly flattens a minority view into the majority cone — these are
not merely technical defects; they are small distortions in the mirror a
civilisation holds up to itself. This is why we refuse to treat any of this
as a black box, why we insisted on building each piece by hand from
weather and typos and coffee: a technology unmoored from the human
understanding of why it does what it does is a technology waiting to
be misused. You are learning the mathematics so that you remain its
author, and never merely its operator.
We came in thinking the transformer was an equation to memorise. We leave
knowing it is a sentence we can speak: a word, unsure of itself, asks its neighbours
who it is, weighs their answers by how much they resonate, and becomes, for one
sentence, certain. That is attention. Everything else is bookkeeping — and the
bookkeeping is where the next three months live.
