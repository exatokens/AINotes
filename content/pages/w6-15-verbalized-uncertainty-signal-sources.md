Once you decide to mark epistemic status — grounded, inferred, unknown — you have to decide *how* to say it out loud, and the obvious answer turns out to be wrong on both ends.

## Core intuition

A system can communicate uncertainty only if it has a real signal underneath the words. Otherwise, it is merely dressing a guess in careful language.

## Why it matters

The value of a hedge is not in sounding cautious; it is in reflecting a measurement that the user can trust. Without this, uncertainty becomes a theater of humility.

## Instructor framing

The "confidently-wrong hedge" warning is the single most important sentence on this page and the easiest one for a team under deadline pressure to skip past. Make the point concrete: a hedge is a promise about a measurement, and shipping hedge language without wiring it to a real signal is worse than shipping no hedge at all, because it actively teaches users to trust a vocabulary that turns out to be decorative.

## Worked example

A support system retrieves three chunks for "does the warranty cover water damage?" and the top chunk scores 0.85 with a groundedness residue of 0.05 — the sentence generated matches the retrieved clause almost word for word. The hedge selector should say "The sources directly state…" Now change only the groundedness residue to 0.4, leaving retrieval untouched: the model has drifted from the clause while writing, adding an inference the sources don't quite carry. Same retrieval, same topic, different output: "The documents suggest, but do not fully confirm…" The wording changed because a *measured* signal changed, not because the model felt less sure — which is exactly the property a confidence number bolted onto the end of an answer can never have, since that number is generated by the same fluent process that produced the possibly-wrong claim in the first place.

We now move from refusing to speaking honestly. The best uncertainty wording is not the most expressive one; it is the one tied to a measurable signal and a repeatable template.

## Why a confidence number fails

The naive approach — ask the model to append a score, "(confidence: 0.72)" — fails twice over. Users don't read numbers as calibrated probabilities; a "72%" reads as either suspiciously precise or meaninglessly vague, and in neither case does it change what the reader does next. And the model's self-reported number is itself uncalibrated: a language model asked how sure it is will cheerfully report high confidence on a fabrication, because fluency and certainty share a surface in the training data. This is the deep reason the uncertainty signal cannot be the model's self-report.

## Verbalized uncertainty — templated, not improvised

The better instrument is **verbalized uncertainty**: templated hedging language, tied to an underlying signal, that tells the reader what to do rather than what to compute:

- *"The sources directly state…"* — for grounded claims
- *"The documents suggest, but do not confirm…"* — for inferences
- *"I could not find support for…"* — for the silent regions

The point is that it's **templated and not improvised**. Improvised hedges drift — the same epistemic status comes out as "probably" one time and "it is likely that" the next, and the reader can't learn to read them. A fixed vocabulary, each mapped to a threshold on a real signal, becomes a language the user can learn: after a dozen answers they know "suggest" means the system found a plausible but uncited basis.

> The subtle danger is the **confidently-wrong hedge**. A hedge is only honest if the signal beneath it is real; a system that says "the documents suggest" when the documents are in fact silent has not been humble — it has laundered a fabrication through the grammar of humility, which is worse than a bare claim because it borrows the credibility of a caveat it did not earn.

## Where the uncertainty signal actually comes from

If not the model's self-report, then what? Four sources are available in production, and they stack — a small Swiss-cheese of uncertainty estimators, not one oracle.

| Signal | What it measures | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Retrieval thinness** | how much relevant evidence came back at all — a query whose top chunks sit below the relevance floor, or whose scores fall off a cliff after the first hit, is barely covered by the corpus | cheapest — check before the generator writes a token |
| **Groundedness residue** | the direct signal from Act II's faithfulness machinery: the fraction of decomposed claims that failed to ground, per-claim so humility can be graded | as cheap as Tier 2 of the escalation ladder |
| **Consistency across samples** | sample the answer several times and measure claim agreement — fabrications are unstable, varying across samples, while grounded claims stay stable (SelfCheckGPT needs no evidence set at all, only the model's own variance) | moderate — multiple generations |
| **Semantic entropy** | the sharpest sample-based signal; ordinary token-level entropy conflates uncertainty about *meaning* with mere uncertainty about *wording* — semantic entropy first clusters sampled answers by meaning (using NLI to decide when two samples say the same thing), then measures entropy over the clusters, invariant to paraphrase | 5-10× sampling cost; cheap probes trained to read it off hidden states push the cost toward zero |

```python
# toy retrieval-thinness + groundedness-residue based hedge selector
def choose_hedge(top_score: float, groundedness_residue: float) -> str:
    # top_score: relevance of the best retrieved chunk (0-1)
    # groundedness_residue: fraction of claims that failed to ground (0-1)
    if top_score < 0.3:
        return "I could not find support for this in the available documents."
    if groundedness_residue > 0.3:
        return "The documents suggest, but do not fully confirm, the following:"
    return "The sources directly state:"

print(choose_hedge(top_score=0.85, groundedness_residue=0.05))
print(choose_hedge(top_score=0.85, groundedness_residue=0.4))
print(choose_hedge(top_score=0.15, groundedness_residue=0.0))
```

None of these four signals is calibrated out of the box — a groundedness residue of "0.3 ungrounded" is not a probability of error; it's a raw score whose relationship to actual error rate is unknown and drifts with the domain, retriever, and model version. Turning a raw score into a decision you can put a guarantee on is the job of the next page — the most principled idea in the Act.

## Math explained step by step

Formalize why picking a hedge is a threshold decision over two signals, not a free-text writing choice.

**Step 1 — treat each signal as an independent gate, not a blend.** `choose_hedge` never averages `top_score` and `groundedness_residue` into one number; it checks them in sequence. That's deliberate: a thin-retrieval failure ($top\_score < 0.3$) and a fluent-but-ungrounded generation ($groundedness\_residue > 0.3$) are different failure modes with different fixes (better retrieval vs. a stricter generator), and collapsing them into one blended score would hide which one occurred.

**Step 2 — the thresholds are the whole policy.** Given the code's cutoffs, there are exactly three reachable outputs: below the retrieval floor, above the residue ceiling, or neither. Moving either threshold trades false hedges for missed ones — raise $0.3 \to 0.5$ on `top_score` and more borderline-but-adequate retrievals get needlessly hedged; lower it and more genuinely thin retrievals get answered as if solid. This is the same precision/recall tension seen at every gate in the course, applied to language instead of admission.

**Step 3 — verify against the worked example above.** $(top\_score, residue) = (0.85, 0.05)$ fails both conditions, falling through to `"The sources directly state:"`. Change only the residue to $0.4$: the second condition now fires, returning the "suggest" template — one flipped threshold, one flipped sentence, and nothing about the retrieval changed. That direct, single-cause traceability is what makes the hedge a *report of a measurement* rather than a matter of the model's mood.

**Step 4 — why the template, not the number, is the output.** The function returns a fixed string, never the raw floats. A user sees "The sources directly state" or "I could not find support for this," never "0.85/0.05" — the signal drives the decision, but the decision is what gets spoken, which is exactly the fix for the confidence-number failure mode described above.

## Practical pattern

Wiring a verbalized-uncertainty layer into a generation pipeline:

1. compute the uncertainty signals (retrieval thinness, groundedness residue, and optionally consistency/semantic entropy) *before* or immediately after generation, never derive them from the model's own self-report of confidence;
2. fix a small, closed vocabulary of hedge templates in advance — three or four sentence patterns, each mapped to a threshold range on the signal — and never let the generator improvise its own hedging language;
3. route every claim through the same template regardless of topic, so a user builds a stable mental model of what "suggest" versus "directly state" means after a handful of interactions, rather than re-learning the vocabulary on every answer;
4. log which template fired alongside the raw signal values, so a drop in answer quality can be traced back to a specific threshold rather than argued about in the abstract.

## Common traps

- letting the generator write its own hedge in free text ("I'm fairly confident that…") instead of selecting from a fixed template — this reintroduces exactly the self-report miscalibration the whole page exists to route around;
- using a single blended confidence score instead of separate signals for separate failure modes, which hides *why* an answer was hedged and makes the threshold impossible to tune sensibly;
- shipping hedge language before the underlying signal is wired up — a "the documents suggest" that isn't actually gated on a measured residue is the confidently-wrong hedge, and it is worse than no hedge at all because it borrows the credibility of a caveat it did not earn;
- letting hedge wording drift across releases or engineers (different phrasings for the same epistemic status), which destroys the "language the user can learn" property that makes a template valuable in the first place.

## Takeaways

- A hedge is a promise about a measurement, not a tone — the words are only as honest as the signal wired underneath them.
- Use a small, fixed vocabulary of hedge templates gated on real signals (retrieval thinness, groundedness residue), never a free-text confidence number or the model's own self-report.
- Concretely: log the signal value alongside every hedge choice, so a wrong hedge can be traced to a specific threshold rather than blamed on the model's judgment.
