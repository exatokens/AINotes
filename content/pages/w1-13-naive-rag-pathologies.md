We can now assemble the simplest possible RAG system — and watch the geometry studied this week predict exactly how it fails.

## Core intuition

The simplest RAG system is not a failure because it is “bad” in some vague sense. It fails because every component is doing the most direct, least disciplined version of the task, while the geometry and the evidence requirements are much stricter than that.

Once you understand the geometry of embeddings, the catalog of naive RAG pathologies becomes almost inevitable.

## Why it matters

This chapter matters because it turns abstract geometry into an engineering checklist. The issues are not random bugs; they are recurring failure modes generated by the assumptions behind naive embedding search and prompt-only generation.

A system that ignores these pathologies will appear to work on friendly examples and fail badly on real tasks.

## Instructor framing

This is the pivot point where the course stops being a theory lecture and becomes a design document. The book is telling you: if you understand the geometry, you can anticipate exactly why a simple retrieval stack will break.

## Worked example


Suppose a question is ambiguous, the evidence is split across chunk boundaries, and the top-k retrieved chunks all sit close to the same noise floor. A naive RAG system will still generate a confident answer because the generator is not checking whether the evidence actually supports it.

The failure mode is predictable because the retrieval stage is weak and the answer stage has no grounding step.

## The three-line recipe

1. Embed every chunk of every document and store the vectors.
2. At query time, embed the question and take the top-$k$ nearest chunks.
3. Paste them into the prompt and let the LLM answer.

```python
# naive RAG in ~15 lines — a fine first prototype, a poor production system
def naive_rag(question, chunks, chunk_vecs, embed_fn, llm_fn, k=4):
    q_vec = embed_fn([question])[0]

    scored = []
    for text, vec in zip(chunks, chunk_vecs):
        scored.append((cosine(q_vec, vec), text))
    scored.sort(reverse=True)
    top_k = [text for _, text in scored[:k]]

    prompt = "Context:\n" + "\n\n".join(top_k) + f"\n\nQuestion: {question}"
    return llm_fn(prompt)   # no check that top_k is actually relevant!
```

It is a fine first prototype and a poor production system. The reasons trace directly back to Acts II and III.

## The catalogue of pathologies

| Pathology | What happens | Geometric root | Fixed by (later week) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Semantic near-misses** | Top-$k$ "nearest" chunks are only marginally nearer than irrelevant ones; the generator states them confidently | Anisotropy compresses the space, concentration thins the margin | Re-ranking cascade |
| **Polysemy collisions** | A query about one sense of a word retrieves chunks about another (the river bank when you meant the financial one) | Representations not context-preserved enough | Query transformation, better embedders |
| **Lost in the cut** | A fact split across a chunk boundary is unretrievable | No embedding can recover information destroyed at ingestion | Chunking (Week 3) |
| **Lexical blindness** | A rare identifier — part number, statute, gene — carries little semantic signal and is missed by dense search | Dense embeddings compress rare tokens away | Hybrid retrieval |
| **No re-ranking** | Matches concentrate near the noise floor; first-pass similarity is too blunt to order survivors well | Thin margin above concentration's noise floor | The re-ranking cascade |
| **Garbled questions** | A misspelled, contextless, or multi-hop question embeds to the wrong place before retrieval even begins | Query embedding is only as good as the query text | Query transformation |
| **Ungrounded answers** | Nothing checks that the generated answer is actually supported by the retrieved evidence | No verification step exists in the naive loop | Guardrails and grounding |
| **Unmeasured** | Nothing tells us which of these is hurting us; every fix is a guess | No metrics anywhere in the pipeline | Evaluation |

## Why this table is the whole course, compressed

Each pathology above is an instrument waiting to be built — this is the promise of the course made concrete. The fourteen instruments are not a grab-bag of techniques; they are a **precise, point-by-point response** to the ways naive RAG breaks, and every one of those breaks is a direct consequence of a geometric fact met this week: the curse of dimensionality, concentration of measure, and anisotropy.

> The strangeness of high dimension is not academic. It is the mechanism behind every failure of naive RAG, and the justification for every instrument we will build. Understand the geometry, and the syllabus stops looking like a list and starts looking like an argument.



## Math explained step by step

Naive RAG's failure is a direct arithmetic consequence of the three geometric facts from this week, not a separate bug. Walk it through:

**Step 1 — define the noise floor.** From concentration of measure, unrelated embeddings already sit at a cosine similarity near some baseline $b$ (close to $0$ in an isotropic space, but pulled higher by anisotropy — previous two pages). Call $b$ the *noise floor*: the similarity score you get essentially for free, from chunks that have nothing to do with the query.

**Step 2 — define the margin.** A genuinely relevant chunk scores $s(q,d)$ somewhere above $b$. The **margin** is $s(q,d) - b$ — the amount by which a true match clears the noise floor. Naive RAG's `top_k` line in the code above just sorts by raw $s(q,d)$ and takes the top few, with no notion of margin at all.

**Step 3 — see why "top-k" alone is not enough.** If the margin is wide (a very on-topic chunk vs. a wildly unrelated one), top-$k$ by raw score works fine — this is why naive RAG looks great on demo questions. If the margin is thin (anisotropy has compressed the space, or the true answer is split across a chunk boundary so *no* chunk scores well), top-$k$ returns chunks that are only *marginally* above the noise floor, and the LLM — which has no way to see $b$ or the margin, only the text it's handed — treats them as authoritative anyway.

**Step 4 — the fix has to be a second, independent judgment.** Because a single cosine score cannot tell "high score because truly relevant" apart from "high score because anisotropy inflated the floor," the later instruments (re-ranking, hybrid lexical+dense scoring, grounding checks) all work by adding a *second, differently-biased* measurement rather than trusting the first score harder. That is the mathematical reason the pipeline grows more stages instead of just tuning $k$.

## Practical pattern

The pathologies above are not a list to memorize; they are the design requirements for the rest of the course. Every later instrument — chunking, hybrid search, reranking, query transformation, grounding, evaluation — is a response to one or more of these failure modes.

## Common traps

- assuming top-k retrieval is a complete answer to relevance;
- omitting chunk-boundary and evidence checks;
- ignoring that retrieval can fail on rare identifiers and ambiguous entities;
- building a system without observability or evaluation.

## Takeaways

- Naive RAG works as a prototype, not as a production system.
- Geometry predicts the failure modes.
- Every instrument in the course is a repair for a specific pathology.
- A good retrieval system is as much about structure and discipline as it is about embedding quality.
